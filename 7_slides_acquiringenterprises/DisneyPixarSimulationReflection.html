Five and a Half Billion of Goodwill

The case

Late 2006. The deal closed on 5 May: Disney bought Pixar all-stock for $7.5 billion. About $1.1 billion was Pixar's own cash, only about $1.0 billion landed on identifiable assets, and about $5.5 billion became goodwill. Source: FY2006 10-K.

Group Discussion

  • Form groups with classmates who had the same role.
  • Before any discussion of answers, draw the Integration Ladder — preserve, symbiosis, absorb.
  • Ask first whether the allocation says this deal bought assets or people, then name your rung.
  • Select one representative to present your group's placement and draw it on the board.
💰

Financial Lead

The price was $7.5 billion. About $1.1 billion was Pixar's own cash, only about $1.0 billion landed on identifiable assets, and about $5.5 billion became goodwill. Was this paid the right way, and what test tells you next year whether it was worth it?

  • A Pay in stock so Pixar's people carry the risk with us; judge it in a year on whether the film-makers are still here and producing.
  • B Pay in stock, manage dilution with buybacks; judge it on whether animation earnings cover the cost of the shares issued.
  • C Borrow and pay cash — cheaper than issuing equity at this price if we're confident.
  • D Renegotiate the price down toward the $1 billion of assets we can actually identify.
💰

Financial Lead — Key Takeaways

  • Convert a cultural argument into a measurable test. “Are the film-makers still here and producing” is something you can check in twelve months.
  • About 73% of the price is goodwill. What Disney bought is not on the balance sheet in any form you can point at.
  • (D) is the trap. It misunderstands the deal entirely. The goodwill is the asset; valuing only what can be listed values a car park.
📣

Marketing Lead

You now own two of the most trusted brands in family entertainment. The filing says the company “now produces feature animation films under both the Disney and Pixar banners.” What do you do with the two names?

  • A Keep them separate and distinct — Pixar films stay Pixar films.
  • B Put both names on the films so trust runs in both directions.
  • C Keep Pixar prominent for now, let it merge into Disney over several years.
  • D Fold Pixar into the Disney brand now — we paid for it, put our name on it.
📣

Marketing Lead — Key Takeaways

  • A brand is a prediction audiences make, not a logo. Two trusted names make two different predictions, and merging them averages the two.
  • The filing's own language — “under both the Disney and Pixar banners” — already contains the structure of the answer.
  • (D) is the trap. “We paid for it, put our name on it” spends $5.5 billion of goodwill on a logo change, and the weaker prediction survives the merge.
⚙️

Operation Lead

Pixar is a wholly-owned subsidiary in Emeryville, four hundred miles from Burbank, with its own building, processes and leadership — while Disney's own animation studio is struggling. How do you integrate?

  • A Leave Pixar where it is and put its leaders in charge of Disney Animation too.
  • B Keep them separate but deliberately share technology and practices both ways.
  • C Keep Emeryville, but have Pixar report through existing studio management.
  • D Move Pixar to Burbank under our animation leadership — that's where the savings are.
⚙️

Operation Lead — Key Takeaways

  • Pick the rung from the allocation, not from instinct. A goodwill-dominant deal sits on preserve.
  • The acquirer's own animation studio is the one that is struggling — which inverts the usual direction of an integration.
  • (D) is the trap. The textbook synergy play. It relocates about $1 billion of assets and destroys the $5.5 billion that cannot be relocated.
💻

Tech Lead

Both studios have a complete animation pipeline, and the filing names “embracing leading-edge technologies” as a reason for the deal. Neither is obviously worse, and running two is expensive. What happens?

  • A Leave Pixar's pipeline alone; move the good parts of it into Disney Animation.
  • B Run both, share tools deliberately where each is genuinely better.
  • C Pick one standard and migrate everyone over about three years.
  • D Migrate Pixar onto our existing systems — two pipelines is duplication.
💻

Tech Lead — Key Takeaways

  • A pipeline encodes a working method, not just software. Standardising the tool standardises the method along with it.
  • Intangibles were only $233 million of the $7.5 billion price. The technology was never what Disney was buying.
  • (D) is the trap. Presented as an IT saving; actually a modification to the process the company paid $5.5 billion of goodwill for.
🔒

Security Lead

About $5.5 billion of what you bought is goodwill — not a building, a patent or a film library. Much of it walks out of the building every evening. What is the top risk, and what do you do about it?

  • A Creative leadership walking out is the top risk — protect their autonomy and how they work.
  • B Lock the intellectual property down — characters and rendering technology are what we bought.
  • C Retention packages and non-competes for the key people.
  • D Standard post-merger security and systems consolidation — routine integration.
🔒

Security Lead — Key Takeaways

  • Recognise the one risk that cannot be contracted away. Retention money keeps people at their desks; it does not keep them doing the work you bought.
  • The strongest control available here belongs to Operations, not Security — saying so plainly is the strong answer, not a dodge.
  • (D) is the trap. It treats a goodwill-dominant creative acquisition as an IT integration. The consolidation itself is the risk.
🧭

Account Manager

You do not answer a functional question. After all five leads present their selection and a one-sentence defence:

  • 1 Rank the five problems 1–5, each used once, with one sentence justifying #1 that contains a figure from the packet.
  • 2 Weight them — 100 points across the five, in blocks of 10, totalling exactly 100.

Part 3 — Place the deal on the ladder. Preserve, symbiosis, or absorb — and what in the numbers tells you so?

🧭

Account Manager — Key Takeaways

  • Ranking is order; weighting is magnitude. A flat split scores 2 of 6 — the designed penalty for refusing to choose.
  • The strong pattern ranks integration and key people above brands and pipeline, keeps the price off the bottom, and weights the top two at about 70 of 100.
  • The deal is PRESERVE, and 73% goodwill is the evidence — ideally noticing the $26 million of abandoned sequels as the sign Disney had already decided.

Key takeaway

Price is standalone value plus synergy, minus the value you destroy integrating. When 73% of the price is goodwill, the integration plan is the deal.

Most of what Disney bought walks out of the building every evening.
