Five and a Half Billion of Goodwill

The case

Late 2006. The deal closed on 5 May: Disney bought Pixar all-stock for $7.5 billion. About $1.1 billion was Pixar's own cash, only about $1.0 billion landed on identifiable assets, and about $5.5 billion became goodwill. Source: FY2006 10-K.

Group Discussion

  • Form groups with classmates who had the same role.
  • Draw the Integration Ladder — preserve, symbiosis, absorb.
  • Ask first whether the allocation says this deal bought assets or people, then name your rung.
  • Cite a figure from your packet, name what your choice gives up, and state one thing that would change your answer.
  • Select one representative to present your group's placement and reasoning on the board.
💰

Financial Lead

The price was $7.5 billion. About $1.1 billion was Pixar's own cash, only about $1.0 billion landed on identifiable assets, and about $5.5 billion became goodwill. Was this paid the right way, and what test tells you next year whether it was worth it?

  • A Borrow and pay cash — it's cheaper than issuing equity at this price if we're confident.
  • B Pay in stock so Pixar's own people carry the risk with us, and judge this in a year on whether the film-makers are still here and still producing.
  • C Pay in stock, manage the dilution with buybacks, and judge it on whether animation earnings cover the cost of the shares we issued.
  • D Go back and negotiate the price down to something closer to the $1 billion of assets we can actually identify.
💰

Financial Lead — Key Takeaways

  • Connect payment to risk. Explain how the financing choice distributes risk between buyer and seller and affects what each expects after closing.
  • Read the allocation before choosing a measure. Identify the sources of value your plan relies on and the evidence that would show whether they persist.
  • Test the acquisition equation. Separate standalone value, anticipated synergy and integration costs. State which assumption matters most to your assessment.
📣

Marketing Lead

You now own two of the most trusted brands in family entertainment. The filing says the company "now produces feature animation films under both the Disney and Pixar banners." What do you do with the two names?

  • A Keep Pixar prominent for now and let it merge into Disney over several years.
  • B Fold Pixar into the Disney brand now — we paid for it, we should put our name on it.
  • C Keep them separate and distinct — Pixar films stay Pixar films, and the name keeps meaning what it means.
  • D Put both names on the films so the trust runs in both directions.
📣

Marketing Lead — Key Takeaways

  • Brand decisions are integration decisions. Explain how your proposal changes audience expectations, the relationship between the studios and the value the acquisition is intended to create.
  • Specify the expected synergy. Identify what the two names might accomplish together and what could be lost in changing their relationship.
  • Choose evidence beyond visibility. State how you would assess audience response and when that evidence would make you revise the branding plan.
⚙️

Operation Lead

Pixar is now a wholly-owned subsidiary in Emeryville, four hundred miles from Burbank, with its own building, processes and leadership — while Disney's own animation studio is struggling. How do you integrate?

  • A Move Pixar down to Burbank under our animation leadership — that's how you get the savings we promised.
  • B Leave Pixar exactly where it is and put its leaders in charge of Disney Animation as well — we bought the way they work, so let it spread.
  • C Keep them separate but deliberately share technology and some practices in both directions.
  • D Keep Emeryville, but have Pixar report through our existing studio management like any other unit.
⚙️

Operation Lead — Key Takeaways

  • Locate the source of value. Use the allocation and operating evidence to explain what the combined company must retain, share or change.
  • Translate a rung into responsibilities. Describe who decides, which processes interact and what the studios would do differently under your proposal.
  • Include the cost of integration. Consider disruption and implementation effort alongside savings. Identify a signal that would challenge the plan.
💻

Tech Lead

Both studios have a complete animation production pipeline, and the filing names "embracing leading-edge technologies" as a reason for the deal. Neither pipeline is obviously worse, and running two is expensive. What happens?

  • A Pick one standard and move everyone onto it over about three years.
  • B Leave Pixar's pipeline alone and move the good parts of it into Disney Animation — not the other way round.
  • C Migrate Pixar onto our existing systems — two pipelines is a duplication we're paying for twice.
  • D Run both and share tools deliberately where each is genuinely better.
💻

Tech Lead — Key Takeaways

  • Tools and work practices interact. Explain how a pipeline change affects production, collaboration and the people using it, as well as software costs.
  • Test the claimed synergy. Specify the benefit of sharing or consolidating tools and the effort needed to achieve it.
  • Make the transition measurable. Identify a trial, a success measure and a condition for revising the plan before wider rollout.
🔒

Security Lead

Roughly $5.5 billion of what you bought is goodwill — not a building, a patent or a film library. Much of it walks out of the building every evening. What is the top risk, and what do you do about it?

  • A Lock the intellectual property down properly — the characters and the rendering technology are what we bought.
  • B Run the standard post-merger security and systems consolidation — this is a routine integration.
  • C Treat the creative leadership walking out as the top risk — protect their autonomy and how they work, because no contract can replace them.
  • D Put retention packages and non-competes in place for the key people.
🔒

Security Lead — Key Takeaways

  • Identify what is exposed. Explain which sources of acquired value are vulnerable and how your proposed controls address them.
  • Assess control limits. Distinguish what a contract or technical safeguard can secure from what depends on working relationships and incentives.
  • Coordinate across roles. Identify who can act on the risk and how a control might affect the integration, technology or brand decisions.

Account Manager

You do not answer a functional question. After the five leads present, consider integration, key people, the price, brands, and the pipeline.

  • 1 Rank the problems 1–5 by what determines whether the $7.5 billion was worth spending. Use each once. Justify #1 in one sentence containing a figure from your packet.
  • 2 Allocate 100 points across the five problems in blocks of 10, totalling exactly 100.
  • 3 Place the deal on the ladder. Preserve, symbiosis, or absorb? Explain what in the numbers supports your choice.
🧭

Account Manager — Key Takeaways

  • Use the allocation as evidence. Explain your rung on the ladder through the sources of value and the assumptions behind the expected synergy.
  • Evaluate the combined plan. Ranking sets order; weighting expresses relative emphasis. Show how the five choices support or undermine one another.
  • Define a future assessment. Choose indicators of value retained, value created and integration cost. Judge from the case evidence rather than the deal's later reputation.

Key takeaway

Read the purchase price allocation, identify the value the buyer expects, then evaluate the integration plan. Explain the standalone value, synergy and integration costs your decisions depend on.
