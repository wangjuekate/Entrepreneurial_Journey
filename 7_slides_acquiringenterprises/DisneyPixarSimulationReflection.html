Five and a Half Billion of Goodwill

Disney / Pixar: preserving the capabilities behind the acquisition.

Share information   /   Make a coordinated decision   /   Explain the priorities

The 2006 deal — $7.5 billion for Pixar

  • Disney announces it will buy Pixar for $7.5 billion in stock.
  • Pixar is a few hundred people in a building in California. No theme parks. No television networks. Some computers, some office furniture, and a rendering system.
  • Now do the autopsy, from Disney's own annual report.

Where the $7.5 billion went

Pixar's own cash and investments — ~$1.1B

Film costs, buildings, equipment, intangibles — ~$1.0B

Goodwill — ~$5.5B

Goodwill on Disney's balance sheet went from $16,974M to $22,505M essentially overnight.

Seventy-three percent. Disney paid roughly a billion dollars for a studio and five and a half billion for a group of people and the way they worked together.

Stage 1 — Key takeaways

Identify what must survive the deal

Acquiring Enterprises: Choose integration around the capability being acquired.

  • Combine budget and retention. The CFO sets a 70-unit budget and at least 90% key-creative retention in year one. The best integration option starts at 95%, leaving only five points of headroom.
  • Connect leadership to technology. Pixar’s leaders running Disney Animation (30) plus Pixar’s pipeline flowing into Disney (25) cost 55. The pipeline loses no retention only under that leadership choice.
  • Reserve room for the brand. Keeping the brands separate costs 15 and loses no retention. It leaves exactly 55 for integration and the pipeline; buyback cash adds nothing to the budget.

Stage 2 — Make Executive Decisions

Chief Financial Officer (CFO)

The price was $7.5 billion, paid in 279 million Disney shares. About $1.1 billion was Pixar’s own cash and investments, only about $1.0 billion landed on identifiable assets — film costs, buildings, intangibles — and about $5.5 billion became goodwill. In the same year Disney repurchased 243 million of its own shares for $6,898 million. Was this paid the right way, and what test tells you next year whether it was worth it?

  • A.Pay in stock, offset the dilution with buybacks, and judge it in year one on whether animation earnings cover the cost of the shares issued.
  • B.Go back and negotiate the price down toward the roughly $1.0 billion of identifiable assets.
  • C.Borrow and pay cash; it is cheaper than issuing equity at this price if we are confident.
  • D.Pay in stock so Pixar’s own people carry the risk with us, and judge it in year one on whether the key film-makers are still here and still producing.

Stage 2 — Make Executive Decisions

Chief Marketing Officer (CMO)

You now own two of the most trusted brands in family entertainment. One is eighty years old; the other has not made a flop. Before the deal both names were already on the films. The filing notes the company “now produces feature animation films under both the Disney and Pixar banners.” What do you do with the two names?

  • A.Put both names on every film so the trust runs in both directions.
  • B.Keep them separate and distinct — Pixar films stay Pixar films, and the name keeps meaning what it means.
  • C.Fold Pixar into the Disney brand now — we paid for it, so our name goes on it.
  • D.Keep Pixar prominent for now and merge it into Disney over several years.

Stage 2 — Make Executive Decisions

Chief Operating Officer (COO)

Pixar is now “a wholly-owned subsidiary.” It sits in Emeryville, four hundred miles from Burbank, with its own building, its own processes and its own leadership. Disney’s own animation studio is struggling. Disney has just abandoned its own Pixar sequel projects and taken a $26 million impairment. How do you integrate?

  • A.Keep the studios separate but share technology and some practices in both directions.
  • B.Keep Emeryville, but have Pixar report through our existing studio management like any other unit.
  • C.Move Pixar down to Burbank under our animation leadership — that is how you get the savings we promised.
  • D.Leave Pixar exactly where it is and put its leaders in charge of Disney Animation as well — we bought the way they work, so let it spread.

Stage 2 — Make Executive Decisions

Chief Technology Officer (CTO)

Both studios have a complete animation production pipeline — rendering, tools, workflow. The filing says the acquisition supports “embracing leading-edge technologies”, yet intangibles were valued at only $233 million. Neither pipeline is obviously worse, and running two is expensive. What happens?

  • A.Migrate Pixar onto our existing systems — two pipelines is a duplication we pay for twice.
  • B.Run both pipelines and share tools deliberately where each is genuinely better.
  • C.Leave Pixar’s pipeline alone and move its good parts into Disney Animation — not the other way round — under Pixar’s leaders.
  • D.Pick one standard and move everyone onto it over about three years.

Stage 2 — Key takeaways

Preserve the creative capability

Acquiring Enterprises: Match the integration approach to the source of value.

  • Make the three choices together. Pixar’s leaders run both studios (30) + separate brands (15) + Pixar’s pipeline into Disney (25) = 70 units at 95% retention.
  • Judge the deal on the relevant horizon. The template favors stock payment and a year-one test of whether key film-makers stay and keep producing. The animation-earnings test is not readable until year three.
  • Check the tempting alternatives. Using both names raises the coordinated plan to 80 units. Sharing practices both ways starts at only 85% retention and also breaks the pipeline’s leadership dependency.

Simulation assumptions: planning units, retention percentages, the board’s test and the pipeline dependency.

Stage 3 — CEO: Set Company Priorities

Chief Executive Officer (CEO)

As CEO, rank the four questions from the one that has to be resolved first for this $7.5 billion to have been worth spending, down to the one that can wait longest. Discuss your executives’ recommendations before setting the company’s priorities. Ask each executive: “What is your recommendation, which colleague’s constraint does it depend on, and why is your question important for the company to resolve?” You receive their answers through team chat, not automatic summaries.

  • The price — what we paid and how we judge it
  • Brands — Disney, Pixar, or both
  • Integration — where Pixar sits and who runs it
  • The pipeline — whose technology survives

Use each question once. Why should your first-ranked question take priority? Refer to information two executives shared, place the deal on the integration ladder (preserve, symbiosis or absorb), and say what in the numbers tells you so.

Stage 3 — Key takeaways

Protect what justified the price

Acquiring Enterprises: Use the integration ladder to explain the priorities.

  • Put Integration and Price first. Either order fits the template: integration protects the creative organization, while the price question establishes how the board judges whether the deal was worthwhile.
  • Place Brands third and Pipeline fourth. The Pixar name is part of the acquired value. The pipeline choice follows the leadership decision and must preserve the way the artists work.
  • Explain the preserve rung. About $5.5 billion of the $7.5 billion price is goodwill in the case. Use evidence from two executives to explain why protecting creative capabilities matters more than forcing uniform systems.
