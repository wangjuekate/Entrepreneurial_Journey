Acquiring Enterprises

MENT 2234 · Module 7 · Concept Lecture

Case: Disney and Pixar · Mental model: the allocation, then the ladder

Buying a company is most of the entrepreneurship that actually happens and almost none of the entrepreneurship anyone talks about. Open on that gap.

There are two ways to become an entrepreneur

  • The one you have all imagined: the garage, the whiteboard, the ramen, the moment it works. Very romantic. Very slow. Most of them die.
  • The one nobody puts in a film: you buy one that already exists.
  • Somebody already did the garage part. Found the customers, hired the staff, survived the first three years. And now they are sixty-three and their kids want to be dentists.

Today

The one thing that decides whether buying a company makes you rich or ruins you.

Shown through the most successful acquisition in modern business history — which, at the time, a lot of very serious people thought was insane.

Ask how many have considered buying a business rather than starting one. Usually nobody. That silence is the reason this module exists.

The car wash — why buy instead of build

  • A friend looked at starting a business for two years. Business plans, spreadsheets, the lot. Then he bought a car wash from a man who had run it for thirty years.
  • Day one he had customers, cash flow, four employees who knew what they were doing, and a building. Day one of a startup you have a laptop and a feeling.
  • What you buy: time — you skip finding out whether anyone wants this. What you pay: the price — the seller already knows it works, so you pay for the certainty.

The trade, in one line

A startup founder pays with years and risk. An acquisition entrepreneur pays with money and debt.

The founder might build something worth a billion. The acquirer probably never has that upside — but they get paid on Friday.

The car wash had a building, four bays and a pressure-washing rig. If everybody quit tomorrow, he would still own something. Hold on to that sentence — it is the entire lecture.

Say the last sentence and then stop. Everything in the next forty minutes is a consequence of it.

What you are actually paying for

Price = standalone value + synergy − integration cost

Standalone value

What the business is worth carrying on exactly as it is.

Synergy

What it is worth extra because you own it. Their product through your sales team. Your factory making their thing.

Integration cost

What it costs to combine them. Systems, redundancies, and about eighteen months where nobody does their actual job.

In every deal anyone regrets, buyers are wildly optimistic about the second number and completely silent about the third. Synergy gets a slide and a dollar sign. Integration cost gets no slide — because admitting it means admitting you will spend a year making both companies worse.

Ask why integration cost never gets a slide. The honest answer — because whoever puts it up is arguing against their own deal — is worth drawing out.

The mental model — the allocation, then the ladder

First read what you bought. Then the ratio tells you what to do about it — you do not get to choose the rung.

Beats 2-4 walk the allocation from the least interesting slice to the most. Land beat 4 hard: goodwill walks out of the car park at 6pm. Beat 6 is the sentence they will need on Thursday.

Goodwill is a beautiful word for an uncomfortable admission

  • After a deal closes, accountants perform an autopsy called the purchase price allocation. They assign the price to everything they can identify: cash, buildings, equipment, patents, inventory.
  • Whatever is left over — the part they cannot assign to anything you can point at — gets a name.
  • Goodwill. It means: we paid this much more than the sum of the things we can list.

What is actually in it

The reputation. The customer habits. The way people work together. The fact that this team keeps producing.

Look at the ratio and you know what kind of deal you just did.

Contrast with the car wash: almost no goodwill. Fire everyone and you still own a car wash. That comparison does more work than any definition.

Three rungs — and the allocation picks one for you

Absorb

When the value is in assets and scale.

Fold it in, cut duplication, bank the savings.

Symbiosis

When the value is in both.

Keep them separate, share deliberately, in both directions.

Preserve

When the value is in people and how they work.

Change as little as possible.

Low goodwill → absorb. High goodwill → preserve.

And here is the cruelty: the deals where the synergies look biggest are exactly the deals where capturing them destroys what you bought.

These are not preferences. Say that twice. Students want to argue integration style as a matter of leadership philosophy; the allocation has already decided it.

January 2006 — $7.5 billion for a building in Emeryville

  • Disney announces it will buy Pixar for $7.5 billion in stock.
  • Pixar is a few hundred people in a building in California. No theme parks. No television networks. Some computers, some office furniture, and a rendering system.
  • Now do the autopsy, from Disney's own annual report.

Where the $7.5 billion went

Pixar's own cash and investments — ~$1.1B

Film costs, buildings, equipment, intangibles — ~$1.0B

Goodwill — ~$5.5B

Goodwill on Disney's balance sheet went from $16,974M to $22,505M essentially overnight.

Seventy-three percent. Disney paid roughly a billion dollars for a studio and five and a half billion for a group of people and the way they worked together.

Let the 73% sit in silence. Then ask which rung. It is not a close call, and it is not a judgement call — the allocation decided it before anyone had a meeting about culture.

The most expensive sentence in the footnotes

  • Disney had already started making its own sequels to Pixar films. In progress. Real money spent.
  • After the deal closed they abandoned them and took a $26 million impairment.
  • They wrote off their own work rather than overrule the studio they had just bought.

Twenty-six million dollars to say one thing

Your judgement outranks our sunk cost.

You will not find that on a slide about culture. You find it in a footnote in the accounts — and for the film-makers deciding whether to stay another decade, that footnote was worth more than any retention bonus anyone could have written.

This is the slide students remember. Preserve is not a mood; it is something a company pays cash for, and here is the receipt.

Why this is hard, honestly

  • You have just spent $7.5 billion. Your board approved it on a presentation with synergies in it. Your shareholders want to know what they got.
  • Every instinct in a large organisation — every process, every incentive, every reporting line — pulls toward absorb. Standardise. Consolidate. Report upward. Capture the savings.
  • So the person arguing for preserve is arguing for spending seven and a half billion dollars and then deliberately not managing it. In a meeting. Out loud. To the people who just wrote the cheque.

Why most acquisitions fail

Not because buyers overpay — they do, and that is survivable.

They fail because the buyer integrates the way buyers integrate, and dismantles the exact arrangement of people and habits that made the thing worth buying.

The value is not destroyed by a bad decision. It is destroyed by a series of completely reasonable ones.

Change tone and level with them. The last line is the one to slow down on — it is also the general lesson of the whole course.

The one line to carry in

Assets arrive on the balance sheet. Value leaves through the car park.

You cannot know what to do with a company you have bought until you know what you actually bought.

So before the integration plan, before the org chart, before the synergy slide — read the allocation. How much of this price is sitting in things that would still be here if everyone resigned on Monday?

When the goodwill is small, integrate hard. When the goodwill is 73% of the price, the most valuable thing you can do is leave it alone.

One rule

You know this deal worked and nobody in 2006 did.

At the time it was a very large cheque for a company with no factory. Defend every choice from what is actually in the filing.

Same no-hindsight rule as Netflix. It matters more here, because the outcome is so famous that students will reason backwards from it without noticing.
