# Buy, Don't Build: Is Acquiring a Business the Safer Path to Ownership, or Just a Different Set of Risks?

Entrepreneurship through acquisition inverts the founding story. Instead of building a company from an idea, you find one that already exists — usually small, usually profitable, often owned by someone approaching retirement — raise capital to buy it, and become its operator. The formalized version is the search fund, in which a "searcher" raises money from a small group of investors to fund the hunt for a target, then raises again to close. The materials from Stanford, the Associated Press, Anthropic, and Open to Debate make a strong case that this path is underrated. They also show that **it substitutes a well-measured set of risks for a poorly-measured one, which is not the same as safe**.

The case for buying starts with what you skip. A startup must discover simultaneously whether the product works, whether anyone wants it, whether it sells profitably, and whether the founder can run an organization. An acquisition answers the first three before you commit: revenue exists, customers exist, unit economics are visible in the financials. What remains is whether you can operate it — a real risk, but one rather than four compounding.

The returns data is unusually good here, because Stanford has tracked it since 1984. As of December 31, 2025, search funds had produced an aggregate internal rate of return of **33.9%** and a return on investment of **4.75x**, with a public market equivalent of 2.88 — consistent outperformance against the S&P 500 across more than 850 core search funds in the United States and Canada. The aggregate acquisition rate since 1996 has been **58%**, acquisition typically takes around **20 months**, and the median purchase price in 2024–25 was **$16 million** (Zhukova, 2026, [Stanford Graduate School of Business](https://www.gsb.stanford.edu/insights/search-funds-keep-offering-proven-path-ownership)).

Read those numbers carefully, because the middle one is the risk. A 58% acquisition rate means **roughly four in ten searchers never buy anything**. They spend two years, funded by investors, hunting for a business at a price they can justify, then return the money. That is the model's modal failure, and it happens before any operating risk begins. Among funds launched between 2021 and 2024 the rate was closer to 50%.

The AP's reporting on Jersey Mike's shows the same logic at institutional scale, and also its catch. Blackstone acquired the chain — grown **"from 857 stores in 2014, to more than 2,800 this year"** — in a transaction valuing it at roughly **$8 billion**, intended to **"accelerate its expansion across and beyond the U.S. market."** Founder Peter Cancro kept a significant equity stake and said **"Blackstone is the right partner to help us reach even greater heights"** (Associated Press, 2024, via [GV Wire](https://gvwire.com/2024/11/19/jersey-mikes-sandwich-chain-is-acquired-by-private-equity-firm-blackstone-for-8-billion/)). Cancro built the company and still owns part of it, but the acquirer now shapes strategy. Buying a business does not resolve the control question; it relocates it. In a search fund the tension runs the other way — the searcher operates the company but answers to the investors who funded both the search and the purchase. **You buy something that works and inherit a governance structure you did not design.**

Financing sharpens this. Acquisitions are usually leveraged, so the business must service debt from day one. A startup with a bad quarter cuts burn; an acquired company may breach a covenant. Leverage rewards competent operation and punishes ordinary variance, removing the tolerance for learning on the job precisely when a first-time operator needs it.

Valuation is where the risk concentrates. In May 2026, with the Council on Foreign Relations, Open to Debate ran "Government as Shareholder: Proactive Competitive Strategy or Last Resort?" Laura Taylor-Kale and Richard Falkenrath argued that government **"can take a proactive stake in critical sectors before crisis, strengthening national competitiveness,"** while Bob Pozen and Yasheng Huang of MIT Sloan defended letting **"markets determine winners and losers while guarding against dependency, inefficiency…and favoritism"** (Taylor-Kale, Falkenrath, Pozen & Huang, 2026, [Open to Debate](https://opentodebate.substack.com/p/government-as-shareholder-proactive)). The dispute is about state equity; the underlying question is the acquirer's: **can a buyer identify strategic value the seller and the market have both mispriced?** If not, you are paying fair value for average returns and calling it entrepreneurship.

Anthropic supplies a concrete screen for that judgment. Massenkoff and McCrory report that **"Occupations with higher observed exposure are projected by the BLS to grow less through 2034,"** with each ten-percentage-point increase in AI coverage corresponding to a 0.6-percentage-point drop in projected growth (Massenkoff & McCrory, 2026, [Anthropic Research](https://www.anthropic.com/research/labor-market-impacts)). Many acquisition targets — bookkeeping practices, insurance brokerages, staffing agencies, professional-services firms — sit squarely in that exposed band. A business with stable historical cash flows can be a poor asset if its core service is being automated, and **historical financials are exactly what an acquisition process is built to examine**.

The most important unanswered question, therefore, is:

**When you buy a business with a proven track record, how do you tell whether you are buying durable value or a well-documented past?**

Due diligence verifies history. It confirms revenue was real, customers paid, margins held. It is far weaker at establishing that those conditions persist, and weakest precisely where a technology is changing what a service is worth. The seller knows more about the future than the buyer does and — structurally, not dishonestly — chose this moment to sell.

The optimistic reading is that most small businesses are sold for reasons unrelated to their prospects: owners retire, partnerships dissolve, families need liquidity. That is why the returns exist — the motive is personal, so the buyer captures value the market never priced.

The pessimistic reading is that owners know their industries better than any buyer can in twenty months, and the timing of a sale is itself information. Telling the retiring owner apart from the one who sees what is coming is the whole skill of acquisition entrepreneurship — and the Stanford data cannot tell you which is across the table.
