Ethical and Legal Standards for Entrepreneurs

MENT 2234 · Module 2 · Concept Lecture

Case: Theranos · Mental model: the Legal × Ethical grid

Do not open with Theranos. Open with the fictional tracker, so they commit to a judgement before they know they are judging a famous company.

The miracle fitness tracker

  • A founder claims her wristband detects dehydration, blood sugar, stress and early illness with 95% accuracy.
  • Investors are excited. Employees work long hours. Customers pre-order.
  • In testing, engineers find many predictions are unreliable. The founder does not say so publicly — she believes it will eventually work.
  • The company keeps advertising the product as fully proven, and starts selling it.

Three questions, before anyone says a company name

What concerns you here?

Who is affected by that decision?

At what point does optimism become dishonesty?

Take answers to the third question and write them on the board. You will come back to those exact words when the grid appears. Nobody has said "Theranos" yet — keep it that way.

Two words we use as if they were one

Legal

Actions that comply with applicable laws, regulations, contracts and formal rules established by society.

Answered by a regulator or a court. Knowable in advance, by asking a lawyer.

Ethical

Actions that are morally responsible, fair, honest and respectful of stakeholders — whether or not any law requires them.

Answered by the people affected. Not always knowable in advance.

Different answers, different judges, different penalties. Legality establishes the minimum. Ethics usually demands more.

Run this as the two open questions from the plan: "what is legal" and "what is ethical", brainstormed on the board first, then reveal each box as the summary of what they said.

Most of you arrive believing these are the same question

  • Payday lending is legal in much of the United States, and few people would call it fair.
  • Recalling a product before a regulator makes you is required by nobody, and is obviously right.
  • Both facts are ordinary. Both are invisible if you only ask one of the two questions.

Getting each one wrong costs differently

Legal, wrong: you lose the company, and sometimes your liberty.

Ethical, wrong: you lose trust, staff and customers — slowly, and then all at once.

Ask: why do organisations focus on legality and neglect ethics? The honest answer — legality has a deadline and a bill, ethics does not — is worth drawing out of them rather than saying.

The mental model — the Legal × Ethical grid

The Venn diagram is easier to use as a 2×2. Two questions, four boxes — and a column down the middle where most new businesses actually live.

Make them draw it. Beat 2 the easy box, beat 3 the obvious one, beat 4 civil disobedience, and slow right down on beat 5 — legal-but-not-ethical is the box they will end up in, not the bottom-left one.

Four boxes, with real occupants

Legal AND ethical

Paying by labour law. Reporting finances accurately. Protecting patient data. Honouring contracts.

Ethical, not legally required

Extra support for a struggling employee. Being transparent about product limits. Recalling before a regulator makes you.

Legal but NOT ethical

Pricing that exploits desperate customers. Confusing terms. Trading on what the customer cannot know.

Neither

Selling illegal drugs. Securities fraud. Here the ethical debate is already over.

The fine-print case: lawyers conclude the advertising complies, because every disclosure technically appears somewhere. Has the company acted legally? Yes. Ethically?

The fine-print scenario is the one to argue about — it is the box most of them will actually be standing in within five years of graduating.

The grey zone is where entrepreneurs actually live

  • Crypto. AI. Early eBay. Gig work. New businesses routinely operate where the law has not arrived yet.
  • That is not automatically wrong. It is the normal condition of innovation.
  • The eBay of 1996 had no settled law about liability for what strangers sold each other. Someone had to go first.

The grey zone has exactly one rule

In the grey zone, the ethical question is the only one you have. Use it — because when the law finally arrives, it will be written by people looking back at what you did.

Head off the cynical reading: this is not permission to do whatever is not yet illegal. The last sentence is the whole point — the law is written retrospectively, by people reading your emails.

The case: Theranos

  • The promise: hundreds of medical tests from a few drops of blood.
  • It attracted major investors, an influential board, enormous media attention and partnerships with healthcare organisations.
  • Investigations eventually showed the technology did not perform as claimed, and that many tests ran on ordinary commercial equipment.

The useful question

Not only what went wrong — but which warning signs should have been acted on, and by whom.

Founder charisma, investor pressure, secrecy, product claims, patient risk, governance. All six are in this case at once.

Point back at the fitness-tracker slide. They already made the judgement; now the company has a name and a share price.

Where Theranos actually sits — all four boxes

Legal and ethical

Keeping the analyzer's design confidential. Every company protects a design.

Legal, not ethical

Being slow to admit test-accuracy problems internally. Ugly — not yet criminal.

The grey zone tipping over

Not telling the retail partner which machines ran the tests. Contract, consent, patient safety.

Neither

Telling investors 2014 revenue was $108 million when it was a little over $100,000. This is what the SEC charged.

Everything above that last card is a judgement call. That last card is not. Students who put the whole company in one box learn nothing from it.

This is the slide that separates a good reflection from a lazy one. Insist they can name which box each decision is in, and why the boxes differ.

The one question the model makes you ask

Ask both, separately, in this order

1. Could a regulator charge me for this?

2. If not — would I be comfortable if the person affected could see exactly what I did?

  • Yes to (1) and the ethical debate is over. You have a legal problem, not a moral one.
  • No to (1) and no to (2) puts you in the bottom-right box — where reputations go to die quietly.
  • The common failure is answering the ethical question and telling yourself you have answered the legal one.
Make them say the order out loud. Reversing it is exactly how smart people talk themselves into the bottom-right box.

The one line to carry in

Legality is the floor, not the standard. And in the grey zone there is no floor — only the answer you will have to give later.

Truthful claims about product, technology, traction and results. Accurate records. Clear accountability when the evidence contradicts the story you have been telling.

Optimism becomes dishonesty at the moment you have evidence and keep selling the old story anyway.

Close the loop on the opening question. That last sentence is the answer to "at what point does optimism become dishonesty" — compare it with what they said at the start.

These are allegations from civil complaints. Argue them as allegations — that is also how you will have to argue in any real room where this matters.
