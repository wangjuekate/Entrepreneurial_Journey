Theranos in 2014: Legal, Ethical, Neither

The case

All figures are quoted from the SEC complaints filed 14 March 2018. These are allegations — Theranos and Holmes settled without admitting or denying them. Say “the complaint alleges.”

Group Discussion

  • Form groups with classmates who had the same role.
  • Before any discussion of answers, draw the Legal × Ethical 2×2 and place your decision in a box.
  • Answer the legal question and the ethical question separately — they are not the same question.
  • Select one representative to present your group's placement and draw it on the board.
💰

Financial Lead

Actual 2014 revenue is a little over $100,000. The investor binder shows over $100 million for 2014 and about $1 billion for 2015. You are raising money right now. What goes in the binder?

  • A The real number — $100,000 — with projections shown separately and clearly labelled.
  • B Projections only for lines with signed contracts behind them; leave the rest out.
  • C No historical figures at all; forward projections with assumptions written beside them.
  • D Present the $100 million as 2014 revenue — we'll get there, and the round closes now.
💰

Financial Lead — Key Takeaways

  • Tense is the tell. “We will make $100 million” is a projection. “We made $108 million” is a statement about a finished year. The first is legal optimism; the second is the charge.
  • (A) takes full credit because optimism about the future is permitted and misdescribing the past is not. (C) is honest in form but evasive — omitting a fact an investor would want is a weaker version of misstating it.
  • (D) is the trap. A hundredfold overstatement of a completed year is a material misstatement, and “we'll get there” is not a defence.
📣

Marketing Lead

Your materials list “current and past clients” including U.S. government and military organisations. What you actually have is a set of discussions. What may you say?

  • A Only relationships we can show a contract for; cut the military language entirely.
  • B “In discussions with” government and military groups; name no deployment.
  • C Keep the aspirational language, add a disclaimer at the front of the binder.
  • D Say the technology is deployed in the battlefield and on medevac helicopters.
📣

Marketing Lead — Key Takeaways

  • Substantiation is the standard: if you cannot produce the contract, you cannot make the claim. Treat a customer claim as a claim of fact, not of ambition.
  • A disclaimer at the front of a binder does not cure a specific false statement inside it. That is the fine-print manoeuvre, and it scores 5 rather than 10.
  • (D) is the trap. A fabricated customer relationship that moves an investment decision sits in the bottom-left box — neither legal nor ethical.
⚙️

Operation Lead

You are expanding into a national pharmacy chain's stores where real patients give blood. The partner believes samples run on your device; most will run on modified machines bought from someone else, and they have not been told. What do you do before you expand?

  • A Tell the partner exactly which tests run on whose machine, before a single new store opens.
  • B Slow the rollout to only the tests your own device is validated to perform.
  • C Keep expanding, but disclose the machine mix to the partner and on the consent form.
  • D Expand on schedule; the machine mix is a trade secret and the results are what matter.
⚙️

Operation Lead — Key Takeaways

  • Sequencing is the whole answer — before the next store opens. The person at risk here is a patient, not an investor.
  • (C) is honest and still scores only 7: it keeps scaling a service whose accuracy is unresolved. Disclosure is not a substitute for validation.
  • (D) is the trap. The partner is contractually entitled to know and the patient bears the risk. This is where a disclosure problem becomes a safety problem.
💻

Tech Lead

Engineers were asked to modify commercially available analyzers to run finger-stick samples the manufacturer never designed them for. Those machines are producing results returned to patients. What do you do?

  • A Validate the modification, document it, disclose the modified use to the regulator and partner.
  • B Stop using them for finger-stick; run finger-stick only on validated in-house tests.
  • C Keep the modified machines in research only, out of the path of any patient result.
  • D Keep them running and keep them quiet — the other companies' logos would end the story.
💻

Tech Lead — Key Takeaways

  • Modifying a cleared device changes what it legally is. Modification is not automatically wrong; undisclosed, unvalidated modification is.
  • The proprietary analyzer performed about 12 of the 200-plus published tests, and roughly 50 to 60 ran on modified third-party machines. The menu and the device were never the same product.
  • (D) is the trap. “The logos would end the story” is a reason the deception exists, not a justification for it.
🔒

Security Lead

The lab is producing accuracy failures on some tests. You hold patient health data. Legal is using trade-secret protection to limit what outside reviewers can see about the analyzers. Where do you set the bar?

  • A Every failure that reached a patient is a reportable incident; rank by harm, take to the board.
  • B Independent lab audit now, with full access under NDA — trade secrets included.
  • C Fix the failures internally, document properly, keep it in the company for now.
  • D Use trade-secret protection to keep reviewers away until the technology catches up.
🔒

Security Lead — Key Takeaways

  • Separate protecting a design from preventing verification. Both reach for the same legal instrument; only one use of it is legitimate.
  • (A) edges (B) because an audit is a one-off and an escalation process is permanent — it treats a wrong patient result as an incident with a severity rather than an embarrassment.
  • (D) is the trap. Legal form, unethical function — and it is what let everything else continue.
🧭

Account Manager

You do not answer a functional question. After all five leads present their selection and a one-sentence defence:

  • 1 Rank the five problems 1–5, each used once, with one sentence justifying #1 that contains a figure from the packet.
  • 2 Weight them — 100 points across the five, in blocks of 10, totalling exactly 100.

Part 3 — Name the line. Of the five, which is already illegal rather than merely uncomfortable? Say it out loud and say why.

🧭

Account Manager — Key Takeaways

  • Ranking is order; weighting is magnitude. A flat 20/20/20/20/20 scores 2 of 6 — the designed penalty for refusing to choose.
  • Full credit for rank #1 names the $108 million against a little over $100,000, and identifies it as a statement about a completed year rather than a forecast.
  • Two sets of numbers to two audiences — investors shown $108M while the valuation firm got the real figure — is the most damning fact in the packet, and most teams miss it.

Key takeaway

Answer the legal question and the ethical question separately. Some of these decisions are genuinely legal-but-ugly, and saying which is which is the exercise — a team that puts all five in the “neither” box has not used the model.

Tense is the tell. “We will” is a forecast. “We did” is a charge.
