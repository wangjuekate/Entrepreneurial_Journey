Theranos in 2014: Legal, Ethical, Neither

The case

All figures are quoted from the SEC complaints filed 14 March 2018. These are allegations — Theranos and Holmes settled without admitting or denying them. Say “the complaint alleges.”

Group Discussion

  • Form groups with classmates who had the same role.
  • Draw the Legal × Ethical 2×2 and place your chosen action in a box. Identify any uncertainty in your placement.
  • Answer the legal question and the ethical question separately — they are not the same question.
  • Cite a figure or phrase from your packet, name what your choice gives up, and state one fact that would change your answer.
  • Select one representative to present your group's placement and reasoning on the board.

Financial Lead

Actual 2014 revenue is a little over $100,000. The investor binder shows over $100 million for 2014 and about $1 billion for 2015. You are raising money right now. What goes in the binder?

  • A Projections only for lines with signed contracts behind them; leave the rest out.
  • B No historical figures at all; forward projections with the assumptions written beside them.
  • C Present the $100 million as 2014 revenue — we'll get there, and the round closes now.
  • D The real number — $100,000 — with projections shown separately and clearly labelled.
💰

Financial Lead — Key Takeaways

  • Optimism and evidence. The lecture asks when optimism becomes dishonesty. A financial story needs a clear account of what is known, what is assumed, and what remains uncertain.
  • Two separate judgments. Assess the legal basis for an investor communication, then consider honesty and fairness to the people relying on it. Confidence in the business does not answer either question.
  • Pressure and trust. A funding deadline makes the tradeoff urgent. Explain what your choice sacrifices today and how it could affect trust over time.

Marketing Lead

Your materials list "current and past clients" including U.S. government and military organisations. What you actually have is a set of discussions. What may you say?

  • A Say the technology is deployed in the battlefield and on medevac helicopters.
  • B Keep the aspirational language, add a disclaimer at the front of the binder.
  • C Only relationships we can show a contract for; cut the military language entirely.
  • D "In discussions with" government and military groups; name no deployment.
📣

Marketing Lead — Key Takeaways

  • The audience matters. Return to the lecture's fine-print example: what a reader understands is part of the ethical question. Examine the impression created by the whole message.
  • Claims carry responsibility. As in the fitness-tracker example, ask how the evidence available compares with the confidence of the public claim.
  • Use both axes. Explain your legal reasoning separately from your judgment about fairness and trust. What would the person relying on the message need to understand your decision?

Operation Lead

You are expanding into a national pharmacy chain's stores where real patients give blood. The partner believes samples run on your device; most will run on third-party machines, some of which have been modified, and they have not been told. What do you do before you expand?

  • A Keep expanding, but disclose the machine mix to the partner and on the consent form.
  • B Tell the partner exactly which tests run on whose machine, before a single new store opens.
  • C Slow the rollout to only the tests your own device is validated to perform.
  • D Expand on schedule; the machine mix is a trade secret and the results are what matter.
⚙️

Operation Lead — Key Takeaways

  • Include absent stakeholders. The lecture defines ethics through responsibility to people affected. Consider the partner, patients and clinicians, including those who have no voice in the expansion decision.
  • Growth distributes risk. The benefits of speed and the costs of uncertainty may fall on different people. Make that distribution visible when defending your choice.
  • Connect the two axes. Identify the contract, consent and safety questions that need examination, then explain what responsible treatment of stakeholders requires beyond minimum compliance.

Tech Lead

Engineers were asked to modify commercially available analyzers to run finger-stick samples the manufacturer never designed them for. Those machines are producing results returned to patients. What do you do?

  • A Stop using them for finger-stick; run finger-stick only on validated in-house tests.
  • B Keep the modified machines in research only, out of the path of any patient result.
  • C Keep them running and keep them quiet — the other companies' logos would end the story.
  • D Validate the modification, document it, disclose the modified use to the regulator and partner.
💻

Tech Lead — Key Takeaways

  • Innovation and uncertainty. Connect this decision to the lecture's grey zone: identify what is technically uncertain and what legal requirements need clarification.
  • Evidence meets the promise. Revisit the fitness tracker. How should a team evaluate a product claim when its ambitions and current evidence differ?
  • Accountability needs an owner. Technical decisions affect people beyond engineering. Explain who should review the evidence and what new finding would make you reconsider your choice.

Security Lead

The lab is producing accuracy failures on some tests. You hold patient health data. Legal is using trade-secret protection to limit what outside reviewers can see about the analyzers. Where do you set the bar?

  • A Use trade-secret protection to keep reviewers away until the technology catches up.
  • B Fix the failures internally, document properly, keep it in the company for now.
  • C Every failure that reached a patient is a reportable incident; rank by harm, take to the board.
  • D Independent lab audit now, with full access under NDA — trade secrets included.
🔒

Security Lead — Key Takeaways

  • Several responsibilities can coexist. Confidentiality, patient welfare and accountability each matter. Explain how your decision balances them and whose interests might receive less attention.
  • Apply the visibility test. Would you be comfortable if the people affected could see exactly how the decision was made? Consider both what they could see and what they could not.
  • Governance shapes behavior. The lecture asks which warning signs should be acted on, and by whom. Examine how authority, incentives and access to information shape the response to a concern.

Account Manager

You do not answer a functional question. After all five leads present their selection and a one-sentence defence, consider investor numbers, the rollout, compliance, public claims, and the device:

  • 1 Rank the five problems 1–5, each used once, with one sentence justifying #1 that contains a figure from the packet.
  • 2 Weight them — 100 points across the five, in blocks of 10, totalling exactly 100.
🧭

Account Manager — Key Takeaways

  • Priorities express a judgment. Ranking states an order; weighting states relative emphasis. Explain the criteria behind both and the tradeoffs your allocation creates.
  • Classify decisions individually. Use the Legal × Ethical grid for specific actions. Support each placement with evidence and identify any uncertainty that could change it.
  • Leadership connects the roles. Investor pressure, product claims, secrecy and patient risk interact. Explain how one lead's decision affects another and who owns the next step.

Key takeaway

Legality is the floor. Ask the legal question first, then assess honesty, fairness and responsibility to those affected. Support both judgments with evidence; make uncertainty explicit.
