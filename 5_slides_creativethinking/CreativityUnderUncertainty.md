# Creativity Under Uncertainty: Is the Entrepreneurial Skill Having Ideas, or Knowing Which Ones to Test?

Creativity is the entrepreneurial trait everyone claims to want and no one can reliably teach. The usual account treats it as generative — the capacity to produce novel ideas, to see what others miss. The materials from Anthropic, Open to Debate, and *The New York Times* point somewhere else. They describe creativity less as the production of ideas than as the discipline of **running enough cheap experiments to find out which idea was right**. That reframing matters now, because the cost of producing an idea has collapsed while the cost of testing one has not.

Open to Debate staged the sharpest version of the disagreement in December 2023, on the motion "Will AI Kill the Future of the Creative Arts?" Jonathan Taplin, director emeritus of USC's Annenberg Innovation Lab, argued yes: **"Without knowledge of human emotions and experiences, any AI-created art will always lack depth and relatability."** Rebecca Fiebrink of the Creative Computing Institute at the University of the Arts London argued no, holding that **"AI is another tool in an artist's toolkit, and it makes creating more accessible for artists who didn't have traditional training"** (Taplin & Fiebrink, 2023, [Open to Debate](https://opentodebate.substack.com/p/will-ai-kill-the-future-of-the-creative-arts)). Both positions concede the same fact: output is now abundant. They differ on whether abundance is the point.

For entrepreneurs the practical question is narrower than the artistic one. A founder does not need the model to be original; a founder needs to try more things before running out of money. On that measure, Anthropic's research is encouraging and uncomfortable in equal parts.

The encouraging part is that experimentation genuinely increases. Studying quantitative social scientists, Lyttelton, Massenkoff, and Wilmers found that researchers using coding agents start **around 25% more empirical projects** than comparable peers over six months and post roughly **50% more working papers** (Lyttelton, Massenkoff & Wilmers, 2026, [Anthropic Research](https://www.anthropic.com/research/coding-agents-social-sciences)). That is the entrepreneurial loop in a research setting: more attempts, faster feedback, more carried far enough to be judged. Anthropic's internal study found the same pattern in a company, where **"27% of Claude-assisted work consists of tasks that wouldn't have been done otherwise, such as scaling projects, making nice-to-have tools, and exploratory work"** (Huang et al., 2025, [Anthropic Research](https://www.anthropic.com/research/how-ai-is-transforming-work-at-anthropic)).

Note what those numbers describe. Not better ideas — **more ideas surviving contact with reality**. The bottleneck AI removes sits between having a thought and building enough of it to see whether it was any good. That is the bottleneck that matters, because most creative failure is not failure to imagine. It is failure to find out.

The uncomfortable part of the same study is who gets this advantage. **"Only 20% have adopted coding agents,"** the researchers report. Among adopters, **"Twice as many researchers with typically male names use coding agents as those with female names,"** and **"Researchers at top universities are 40% more likely than others to use coding agents."** A tool that multiplies experiments is being taken up first by people who already had the most room to experiment. Creativity under uncertainty is partly a function of how much uncertainty you can afford, and that has never been evenly distributed.

The connection to technological change is more complicated than "AI makes people creative." *The New York Times* reported on the contractors who produce the training data these systems learn from. Edwin Chen, founder of the data company Surge, described the arrangement plainly: **"I often think about us as essentially, like, the school for AGI…AI comes to us, and AI learns to run the world."** Historian Margaret O'Mara supplied the caution: **"The machine is hiding the things that humans are doing"** (Kelley, 2026, The New York Times, via [GV Wire](https://gvwire.com/2026/07/11/the-work-of-helping-ai-destroy-work/)).

That is worth holding onto when a tool feels like it is generating something from nothing. It is recombining human work at a scale you cannot see. Which suggests where an entrepreneur's creative advantage actually sits: **not in generating what the model can generate, but in noticing what is absent from what it produces**. A model trained on the existing distribution of human work is superb at the center of that distribution and unreliable at its edges. The edges are where new businesses are.

Adaptation is the third theme, and Anthropic's usage data captures it as a live shift. In the January 2026 Economic Index, **"The share of conversations classified as augmented jumped 5pp to 52% and the share deemed automated fell 4pp to 45%"** (Appel et al., 2026, [Anthropic Research](https://www.anthropic.com/research/anthropic-economic-index-january-2026-report)). Users are moving toward working *with* the system rather than handing tasks off to it — a learned behavior, arrived at by trial, not a property of the technology.

The most important unanswered question, therefore, is:

**When the cost of producing options falls to nearly zero, how does an entrepreneur decide which options are worth pursuing?**

This is harder than it first appears. Creativity has always been rationed by effort: you could only build a few things, so you thought carefully about which. That constraint was a filter, and it did real work. Remove it and you get a hundred plausible directions, all cheap to start, none obviously wrong, and no principle for choosing. Abundance of options is not clarity about them.

The optimistic answer is that testing replaces judgment — run the experiments and let results decide. But results only decide if you know what you were testing for, and cheap experiments tend to produce ambiguous evidence that supports continuing in every direction at once. The pessimistic answer is that the scarce skill was always taste, that taste comes from having been wrong expensively, and that a founder who has never paid for a mistake has no basis for choosing.

What the readings establish is that creativity is no longer scarce as *output*. The scarce thing is the judgment to look at fifty cheap experiments and see which one is a business — and nothing here suggests that judgment has gotten any cheaper to acquire.
