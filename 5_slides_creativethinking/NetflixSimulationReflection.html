Stage 1 — Key takeaways

Define the problem together

Creative Thinking: Define the problem; know where you are on the loop.

  • Combine the four perspectives. Finance, marketing, operations, and technology each hold part of the problem; consultation makes the constraints visible.
  • Frame the decision: How can Netflix test living-room streaming within its resources while continuing to serve DVD customers?
  • Recognize the stage. DVD is an established product; streaming is still a prototype. Use the evidence available in the simulation, not knowledge of Netflix’s eventual success.

Stage 2 — Key takeaways

Choose a prototype you can learn from

Creative Thinking: Separate diverging from converging; fund a prototype for learning.

  • Consider alternatives before choosing. The simulation supplies options; evaluating them practices convergence. In your own venture, first make time to generate options without judging.
  • Coordinate one feasible experiment. A limited television rollout to existing subscribers needs technology, recruitment, and support together, while DVD service continues.
  • Match expectations to the stage. The rollout should generate evidence about viewing and service needs. Immediate standalone revenue or a full DVD replacement is not the test of a useful prototype.

Stage 3 — Key takeaways

Prioritize the next turn of the loop

Creative Thinking: Prototype and test, then redefine the problem and go again.

  • Prioritize what makes the test possible. The CEO combines executive evidence: device reach and funding shape feasibility; operations supports delivery; positioning must match what the team can provide.
  • Make the next test capable of failing. Before rollout, specify what viewing behavior and support demand would challenge the plan. A priority ranking alone does not test the hypothesis.
  • Use results to revise the problem. After the rollout, compare observations with expectations and adjust the next experiment. Verify shipment changes before counting DVD savings.
