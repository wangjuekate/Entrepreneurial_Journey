Creative Thinking

MENT 2234 · Module 5 · Concept Lecture

Case: Netflix · Mental model: the Diverge–Converge Loop

Tell this like a stand-up set. Pause where the script pauses. The whole hour is a demolition of "creativity is a personality trait" — do not soften it.

Creativity is not a personality

  • You think there are creative people — art students, the one in your group project with opinions about typefaces — and then there is you, doing the spreadsheet.
  • I am going to spend twenty minutes taking that away from you.
  • Creativity is a loop. A process with three steps you can run on purpose, on a Tuesday, in a bad mood, without any tattoos at all.

And at the end

I will show you a company that ran the loop correctly and then almost described it wrong in its own annual report.

Ask for a show of hands: who considers themselves a creative person? Note the number. Ask again at the end of the hour.

The wrong question — a true story

  • 1940s. A Swiss engineer, George de Mestral, comes back from walking his dog in the Alps and spends the evening picking burrs out of its fur. Annoying. Everybody who has owned a dog has done this.
  • Here is what he did differently: he put one under a microscope.
  • He was not asking “how do I get burrs off my dog.” He asked “why does this stick so well.” Tiny hooks catching tiny loops. Eight years later he patented Velcro.

Inspiration did not strike

The dog invented nothing. The microscope invented nothing. The question did.

He swapped a complaint for an investigation, and the idea was on the other side of it.

Pause after "he put one under a microscope". This story is normally told as luck; the whole point is that the only lucky thing was a change of question.

Three steps, and almost nobody does the first one

1 · Define the problem

— and notice you probably defined it wrong.

“Sales are down.” “Students aren't engaged.” “Get the burrs off the dog.” Every one is an answer pretending to be a question.

2 · Ideate

— and stop judging so early.

Generate options without deciding between them. School trained you against this. Judging while you generate is growing a plant while pulling on it to check the roots.

3 · Prototype and test

— cheaply, in a way that can actually fail.

The point is not to prove you were right. It is to find out for as little money as possible whether you were wrong. A test that cannot fail is a performance.

Step 1 is where the marks are. Make them re-ask a problem from their own team venture out loud before you move on.

Why it is a loop and not a staircase

  • You do not run the three steps once and walk away with an idea.
  • The test fails or half-works — and the failure tells you something about the problem you did not know when you defined it.
  • So you define it again, better, and go round.

The Velcro story hides forty tries

The version you hear: dog, microscope, Velcro. The version that happened: eight years of this material doesn't hook, this loom won't weave it, it works but it costs too much.

Every one of those redefined the problem. He did not persist. He iterated.

The mistake that ends careers is not failing. It is finishing one lap and treating the result as final — “it worked, ship it” or “it failed, kill it” — when the honest answer is “that lap taught me the question was wrong.”

Change tone here. This is the part they actually need, and it is the part that shows up in the reflection grade.

The mental model — the Diverge–Converge Loop

Three boxes and one arrow back. The arrow back is the part people leave out — and the part that makes it a model rather than a poster.

Beats 1-3 build the pipeline; beat 4 is the whole point. Beat 5 is the meeting they have all sat in. Land hard on beat 6 — it is the sentence the simulation grades.

Two moves — and you cannot run both at once

Diverge

Doing: generating options.

Right instinct: “yes, and — what else?”

Fails by: never stopping. Feels: wasteful.

Converge

Doing: choosing between them.

Right instinct: “which of these could be wrong?”

Fails by: starting too early. Feels: decisive.

Almost every bad meeting you will sit in is a room trying to do both at the same time. Somebody offers an idea, somebody explains why it will not work, and forty minutes later you have two options and everyone is annoyed.

Do them at separate times, and say out loud which one you are doing. “We're still diverging — hold the objections for ten minutes” is worth more than any creativity workshop you will ever attend.

This is the most immediately usable slide in the course. Tell them to try the sentence in their next team meeting and report back Thursday.

Knowing which stage you are at is the actual skill

The most expensive mistake in business is treating a prototype like a product.

A prototype

Funded for learning. Judged on what you found out. Expected to be embarrassing.

A product

Funded for revenue. Judged on money. Had better not be embarrassing.

Get it backwards and one of two things happens: you cancel an experiment for missing a revenue target it should never have had, or you bet the company on something that was never properly tested. Both look, from the inside, exactly like being decisive.

This is the bridge into Netflix. Say explicitly that the next slide is a company being honest about which stage it was at.

The company you are about to open

  • January 2007. A DVD-by-mail company adds a feature letting subscribers watch films instantly. You know the company. You know how the story ends.
  • Nobody in that building knew.
  • What they actually had: about 6,000 titles instantly, against about 90,000 on disc. One fifteenth of the catalogue.
  • And it only ran on a personal computer — a machine that in 2007 sat on a desk in a study. Ask your parents where they watched films in 2007.

From the same annual report, same paragraph

“We believe that the DVD format, along with its high definition successor formats, including Blu-ray, will continue to be the main vehicle for watching content in the home for the foreseeable future.”

Read the quotation aloud, slowly, and then let it sit in silence. Do not explain it yet — the next slide is the explanation and it is much better if they get there first.

Read it the other way

The lazy reading

A company that did not see it coming.

The useful reading

That is what the prototype stage sounds like when someone is being honest.

  • They had a hypothesis, not a conclusion. Six thousand titles on the wrong screen.
  • So they funded it as an experiment, bundled it free so they could watch what people actually did with it, and kept the disc business at full strength because it was paying for everything.
  • Then they went round the loop again. The living room came later. The catalogue came later. Original content came a lot later.
This is the slide that changes how they read every annual report for the rest of the course. Hedged language is sometimes cowardice and sometimes accuracy — make them argue which this is.

The one line to carry in

Ideas are cheap. Knowing what you have actually got is expensive.

Everyone in the DVD industry in 2007 had the idea that films would eventually stream. It was obvious, and it had been obvious for a decade. The idea was free.

The creative act was knowing what stage they were at — knowing what they had shipped was still an experiment, and having the nerve to keep funding it as one, instead of killing it for missing a number it should never have been given.

Creativity is not a personality. It is a loop, and the skill is knowing where on the loop you are standing.

Ask the opening question again: who is a creative person? The hands should change. If they do not, you rushed the Velcro slide.

One rule, and I am serious about it

You know how this turns out and they did not.

“Streaming obviously wins” is not an argument — it is a spoiler. Defend every choice from what is actually in the filing in front of you.
