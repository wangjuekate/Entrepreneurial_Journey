# Building the New Inside the Old: Why Corporate Entrepreneurship Is Getting Easier and Harder at the Same Time

Corporate entrepreneurship — intrapreneurship — is the practice of creating genuinely new value inside an organization that already exists. The intrapreneur launches products, enters markets, sometimes rebuilds the business model, but does it with someone else's balance sheet, brand, and distribution. That trade is the whole subject: you give up control and speed, and you gain resources and reach. The materials from Anthropic, *The New York Times*, and Open to Debate suggest AI is shifting both sides of that trade at once, which makes the live question **whether the resources large firms offer still outweigh what they cost you**.

Anthropic's study of its own workforce is unusually useful here, because it measures the thing intrapreneurship is supposed to produce: work that would not otherwise have happened. Huang and colleagues found that **"27% of Claude-assisted work consists of tasks that wouldn't have been done otherwise, such as scaling projects, making nice-to-have tools, and exploratory work"** (Huang et al., 2025, [Anthropic Research](https://www.anthropic.com/research/how-ai-is-transforming-work-at-anthropic)). That figure is the intrapreneurial margin made visible: a quarter of AI-assisted output was not a faster version of an existing task but new activity that previously died when someone estimated the effort and decided it was not worth asking for.

This matters because the classic barrier to corporate entrepreneurship has never been imagination. It is **the cost of getting permission**. In most organizations a new idea must be specified, justified, staffed, and budgeted before anyone can see whether it works. That approval cost is fixed, so it dwarfs small ideas — which is why large firms reliably miss opportunities that look trivial at the outset. When an employee can build a working prototype in an afternoon, the sequence inverts: demonstrate first, request resources second. The idea arrives at the meeting as evidence rather than a proposal.

The same study describes the capabilities shifting alongside it. Employees reported becoming more **"full-stack,"** succeeding at tasks outside their normal expertise, with a roughly 50% average productivity increase — more than double the prior year. Engineers increasingly described themselves as **"managers of AI agents,"** some noting their work had shifted **"70%+ to being a code reviewer/reviser rather than a net-new code writer."**

That last shift is double-edged. A person who covers several functions carries a venture further alone before needing the cross-functional team internal ventures usually die waiting for. But a reviewer is not a builder: an organization that mostly reviews output may generate more initiatives while accumulating fewer people who have built anything end to end.

A subtler cost appears in the same findings. **"Claude has become the first stop for questions that once went to colleagues,"** the researchers report, reducing mentorship interactions and changing workplace social dynamics. Intrapreneurship runs on exactly those interactions: internal ventures survive because someone senior takes an interest, provides cover, and knows which vice president to call. A model answers a technical question faster than a colleague, but it cannot sponsor you. **The conditions that make corporate entrepreneurship possible — sponsorship, slack, political protection — are relational, and the efficiency gains fall hardest on relationships.**

Anthropic's adoption data suggests where large firms are actually deploying this capability. Roughly **77% of enterprise API usage exhibits automation patterns**, against about 50% of consumer conversations (Appel et al., 2025, [Anthropic Research](https://www.anthropic.com/research/anthropic-economic-index-september-2025-report)). Enterprises are mostly buying cost reduction rather than exploration. That is rational, and a poor description of entrepreneurship: automating a known process makes an organization better at what it already does, which is precisely the competence that makes incumbents vulnerable.

Whether scale helps or hurts innovation was the explicit subject of an Open to Debate contest in December 2025 at the Johns Hopkins Bloomberg Center. Arguing that the government should break up Big Tech, Bharat Ramamurti and Matt Stoller contended that **"these firms are monopolies that stifle competition and innovation."** Geoffrey Manne and Jennifer Huddleston countered that **"breaking them up would undermine innovation and downgrade the user experience"** (Ramamurti, Stoller, Manne & Huddleston, 2025, [Open to Debate](https://opentodebate.substack.com/p/the-hopkins-forum-returns-to-dc-with)). Strip away the antitrust framing and both sides are making claims about intrapreneurship: one says large integrated firms are where serious innovation happens, the other says they are where it goes to be absorbed and shelved.

*The New York Times* offers the case that pressures both positions. Matthew Gallagher's telehealth company Medvi reached $401 million in 2025 sales and was projected at $1.8 billion with two employees, built on more than a dozen AI tools (Griffith, 2026, The New York Times, via [GV Wire](https://gvwire.com/2026/04/05/a-1-8-billion-company-with-two-employees-ai-made-it-possible/)). If that is achievable outside a corporation, the intrapreneur's calculation changes. The traditional reason to build inside a large firm was that you could not assemble the capability alone. **When you can, the corporation must justify itself on something other than resources.**

The most important unanswered question, therefore, is:

**If AI supplies the capability an employee once needed a corporation to provide, what does the corporation still uniquely offer an entrepreneur — and is it enough to keep the ambitious ones inside?**

Firms retain several things no solo founder has: regulatory standing, existing customer relationships, proprietary data, capital that does not require fundraising, and the ability to absorb a failure without ending anyone's career. These are real, and they are why AWS could be built inside Amazon rather than as a startup.

But the readings suggest those advantages are easy to squander. A firm that uses AI mainly to automate existing workflows becomes efficient without becoming inventive. A firm that lets internal ventures skip the approval bottleneck — while still supplying the sponsorship and protection a model cannot — becomes something a talented person cannot replicate alone.

The question corporate entrepreneurship now poses is not whether employees can innovate. The evidence says they already are, at roughly a quarter of their output. It is **whether the organization notices in time to fund what its people have already started building without asking**.
