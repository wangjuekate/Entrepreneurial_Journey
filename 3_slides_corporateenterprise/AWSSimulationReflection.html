The Venture Inside: AWS in 2008

The case

Early 2008. Amazon has just filed its FY2007 annual report: $14.8 billion of goods sold, $655 million from operations — a 4.4% margin. Eighteen months ago it launched Amazon Web Services, sold to a customer the filing has to introduce as “developer customers.”

Group Discussion

  • Form groups with classmates who had the same role.
  • Before any discussion of answers, draw the CEAI chain — trigger, internal conditions, middle manager, outcome.
  • Place your decision on the chain and say which link it depends on.
  • Select one representative to present your group's placement and draw it on the board.
💰

Financial Lead

Technology and content spend rose from $451 M to $818 M in two years. Operating margin is 4.4%, and AWS revenue is not reported separately anywhere. How does this business get funded and measured?

  • A Its own P&L and capital budget, measured against outside cloud competitors — not retail.
  • B A protected central innovation budget, ring-fenced so retail's targets can't raid it.
  • C Funded out of retail's operating budget, held to the same margin everyone else hits.
  • D No dedicated funding until it shows enough revenue to justify it.
💰

Financial Lead — Key Takeaways

  • Name the benchmark, not just the budget. A venture measured against retail's 4.4% margin will always look like a bad retailer.
  • Technology and content spend nearly doubled, $451 M to $818 M, while AWS revenue appeared nowhere in the filing — the venture was being funded before it could be measured.
  • (D) is the trap. With what would it produce the revenue? This is the single most common way an intrapreneurial venture dies, and it sounds like prudence.
📣

Marketing Lead

The filing introduces “developer customers” who can use AWS to “enable virtually any type of business.” Amazon has never sold to developers. Where does the effort go?

  • A Make it something a developer can try in ten minutes — docs, self-serve signup, free tier.
  • B Go after startups and small companies who could never afford their own data centre.
  • C Sell through the enterprise relationships we already have from seller services.
  • D Market it to the customers we already have — the people who buy books from us.
📣

Marketing Lead — Key Takeaways

  • Separate who chooses from who pays. The developer who picks the service has no budget authority and decides anyway.
  • Amazon had never sold to developers — the filing had to introduce them as a category. A new customer type is a new company, not a new campaign.
  • (D) is the trap. “Leverage our customer base” sounds like synergy. Nobody buying a book wants a message queue.
⚙️

Operation Lead

EC2 lets developers “requisition machines for use” on demand — so you must own the computers before anyone asks. The filing warns that new businesses make the network “increasingly complex.” How much do you build ahead of demand?

  • A Build ahead and accept low utilisation early — no capacity on demand means no product.
  • B Start on spare capacity from the retail peaks, then build dedicated capacity as demand shows.
  • C Lease from existing data centre providers until demand is proven, then build.
  • D Add capacity only after demand appears — don't buy servers on a hope.
⚙️

Operation Lead — Key Takeaways

  • Name the asymmetry between over- and under-building. Idle servers are a cost you can see; the customer who could not get a machine is a cost you cannot.
  • Renting machines “on demand” means owning them before anyone asks. The product promise is a balance-sheet commitment.
  • (D) is the trap. A developer who cannot get a machine does not complain. They leave, and they tell other developers.
💻

Tech Lead

AWS is being built out of the infrastructure Amazon already runs its store on. The filing describes S3 as “a simple web services interface.” What do you build and what do you buy?

  • A Documented services behind one interface — and our own teams use exactly the same one.
  • B Build for outside customers first; move internal teams onto it later, once proven.
  • C White-label infrastructure from an existing hosting provider, our interface on top.
  • D Expose the systems that already run the retail site directly — fastest way to ship.
💻

Tech Lead — Key Takeaways

  • Ask who is allowed to bypass the interface. If internal teams keep a private door, the public one never becomes a product.
  • One interface used by everyone, including the parent, is the only honest test that the product is finished.
  • (D) is the trap. An internal system assumes its callers are colleagues. A product cannot assume anything about its callers at all.
🔒

Security Lead

You will hold other companies' data and run their code on hardware you also use for Amazon's own retail business — and some customers will be Amazon's competitors. Where do you set the bar?

  • A Treat separating customers on shared hardware as the top risk; publish who secures what.
  • B Stand up a full compliance and certification programme now, before a serious customer.
  • C Cap liability in the contract; customers secure their own data.
  • D Defer serious security spend until revenue justifies it.
🔒

Security Lead — Key Takeaways

  • Multi-tenancy is a new, top-severity risk class, not an increment on the old one. You are running competitors' code on the hardware that runs your own store.
  • Publishing who secures what is a product decision as much as a security one — the boundary has to be legible before an enterprise will cross it.
  • (D) is the trap. In infrastructure, trust is the product. Deferring it is deferring the product.
🧭

Account Manager

You do not answer a functional question. After all five leads present their selection and a one-sentence defence:

  • 1 Rank the five problems 1–5, each used once, with one sentence justifying #1 that contains a figure from the packet.
  • 2 Weight them — 100 points across the five, in blocks of 10, totalling exactly 100.

Part 3 — Name what it needs from its parent. Money? Permission? Time? Cover from the quarterly review? Say which, and why.

🧭

Account Manager — Key Takeaways

  • Ranking is order; weighting is magnitude. A flat split scores 2 of 6 — the designed penalty for refusing to choose.
  • The strong pattern ranks funding and the platform above customers and capacity, weighting those two at about 70 of 100 between them.
  • What the venture most needs is rarely money. It is protected resources measured against an external benchmark — permission, expressed in budget form.

Key takeaway

A corporate venture is killed by the parent's metrics long before the market gets a vote. The CEAI chain asks what the venture needs from the organisation around it — and the answer is usually permission, not cash.

Measured against retail, every new business is a bad retailer.
