This is the module that answers "I am never going to start a company, why am I here". Say that out loud in the first minute.

Gmail inside Google

  • In the early 2000s email providers offered tiny storage. People deleted mail to make room for mail.
  • A Google engineer, Paul Buchheit, thought email could be redesigned. He did not leave to found a startup.
  • He built it inside, on the company's resources, infrastructure and expertise. Most people thought 1 GB of free storage was impossible.
  • Google let him experiment, and backed it.

The definition, from the examples

Corporate entrepreneurs are employees who create significant innovations, businesses, products, services or business models while working inside an established organization.

Unlike founders, they have access to organizational resources — and must navigate organizational constraints to use them.

Brainstorm first, reveal the cards as confirmation. If someone names a company you have not heard of, take it — the point is that the category is everywhere.

Two shapes it takes

Corporate venture

A new business, venture, product line or growth opportunity created inside the corporation.

AWS inside Amazon · Gmail inside Google · PlayStation inside Sony

Why build rather than acquire a startup?

Strategic entrepreneurship

Entrepreneurial action that reshapes the company's own strategy, competitive position, capabilities or business model.

Netflix DVD → streaming · Microsoft → cloud · Adobe → subscription

Why is this harder than launching a product?

The second is harder because there is no clean line between the new thing and the thing that currently pays everyone's salary.

Both discussion questions are worth two minutes each. The answer to the second one previews the trap you return to in Module 6, when a fast decision turns out to have been a one-way door.

What an intrapreneur actually does

1 · Recognize

Spot the opportunity — usually from inside the operation, where the pain is.

2 · Develop

Turn a complaint into a concept somebody could fund.

3 · Build support

The hard one. Find the manager who will spend their credibility on it.

4 · Experiment

Validate cheaply, before the number is big enough to be noticed.

5 · Scale and integrate

Additional funding, expanded team, market rollout — and the moment the venture stops being a secret and starts being a line item somebody has to defend.

Stage 3 is where corporate ventures die, not stage 1. Say so now; the figure on the next slide explains why.

The mental model — the CEAI chain

The five stages tell you what an intrapreneur does. This tells you whether any of it survives.

Beat 1: triggers are free. Beat 2: the conditions are the whole game — pause on the absence of money. Beat 3 is the finding they do not expect. Do not rush beat 5; benchmarking twice is what the simulation grades.

The five conditions — and what each one really asks

Management support

Will someone senior spend their own credibility on this?

Work discretion

Can I decide without asking? Am I allowed to be wrong?

Rewards

Does anyone's career get better if this works?

Time availability

Is this someone's job, or their evenings?

Boundaries

Where, exactly, does the permission run out?

Not on the list: money

Amazon had $1.18 billion of free cash flow in 2007. Cash was never the binding constraint.

Save the sixth card. Ask them what is missing from the list before you reveal it — almost every room says "budget", which is exactly the wrong answer.

The pivot is not the CEO

  • Whether a venture gets resources, discretion and air cover is decided by a manager two or three levels down.
  • Senior enough to allocate people. Junior enough to be measured on this quarter.
  • That person is simultaneously the venture's only advocate and the one who pays for it out of their own numbers.

Advantages come with their opposites

You get: capital, talent, an existing customer base, distribution, brand, infrastructure.

You get: approval layers, legacy incentives that reward protecting the old business, internal politics, and risk aversion.

Ask who in the room has worked somewhere with a manager like this. The answers are better than anything you can say here.

Where AWS actually sits on the chain

Trigger

Cheap commodity servers, and the company's own scaling pain. Every competitor saw the same thing.

Management support

Explicit: "we generally focus on growing gross profit and operating profit dollars rather than maximizing margin percentages."

Time and discretion

A dedicated team — not a side project, not someone's evenings.

Boundaries

A 4.4% operating margin, and shareholders who read every quarter.

Middle manager

The person who had to defend the spend at the budget review.

Outcome, year one

Operating income fell from $432M to $389M while technology spend rose from $451M to $662M.

Read the last card again. The venture's cost hits the parent's books a full year before any benefit does. That is not failure — it is the normal shape of every corporate venture, and precisely the shape that gets one cancelled.

This is the slide that decides Thursday. If they internalise the one-year lag, the simulation's budget-review decision becomes arguable instead of obvious.

The one question the model makes you ask

Not “is it a good idea?” — but which of the five conditions does this idea not have yet, and who is the one manager who could grant it?

Ideas are not scarce inside large firms. Conditions are.

And benchmark the outcome twice — against competitors, and against the firm's own prior years. Compare a two-year-old venture only to its parent and it looks pitiful. Compare it only to itself and you never notice it is losing.

Have them apply the question to their own team venture: which condition is missing, and who could grant it. It works for a student team as well as a corporation.

The one line to carry in

Startups create innovation without organizational resources. Corporate entrepreneurs create it despite organizational bureaucracy.

The central challenge is not generating ideas. It is turning an idea into organizational action and sustained value — which is a political skill as much as a creative one.

Return to the opening vote: entrepreneur, corporate entrepreneur, or both. Most rooms move after this slide.

What makes this module different

You are not a startup. You have a parent. The parent has shareholders. And the parent reads results every ninety days.

Hand out role packets. Stress the ninety-day clock — it is the constraint that makes the AWS decisions hard rather than obvious in hindsight.
