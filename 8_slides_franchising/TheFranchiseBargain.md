# The Franchise Bargain: How Much Independence Should You Trade for a System That Already Works?

Franchising asks you to give something up before you start. You supply the capital, sign the lease, hire the staff, and carry the operating risk; in exchange you receive a brand customers recognize, a proven operating model, supplier relationships, and training. You do not decide what to sell, how to price it, or what the store looks like. The materials from the Associated Press, GV Wire, the New York Attorney General's office, Anthropic, and Open to Debate suggest this bargain can be extraordinarily good or ruinous, and that **the difference turns on how much of the system was real**.

Start with the version that works. The Associated Press reported Blackstone's acquisition of Jersey Mike's at roughly **$8 billion**. Behind that price is the model working: the chain expanded **"from 857 stores in 2014, to more than 2,800 this year"** and posted **"sales of $3.3 billion in 2023, up 25% from the prior year"** (Associated Press, 2024, via [GV Wire](https://gvwire.com/2024/11/19/jersey-mikes-sandwich-chain-is-acquired-by-private-equity-firm-blackstone-for-8-billion/)).

Two thousand new locations in a decade requires two thousand people willing to supply capital and labor to a business someone else designed. **Franchising raises capital and management attention at once**, from people who would never fund a startup but will risk their savings on a sign they recognize. The franchisee's side is the mirror image: existing demand, known costs, a playbook.

The costs are equally structural. A franchisee pays an upfront fee, royalties on gross sales, and an advertising contribution — all calculated on revenue rather than profit, so the obligations continue in full through a bad year. The franchisor controls menu, pricing, suppliers, design, and standards, enforced by inspection and the threat of non-renewal, and drafts the agreement setting term length, territory, transfer rights, and termination. **You are buying an operating system and accepting its license terms.**

Labor is where those fixed obligations become acute, because franchisees carry wage costs without the pricing freedom to offset them. Open to Debate staged that tension in April 2013 on the motion "Abolish the Minimum Wage" — James Dorn of Cato and Russell Roberts of Hoover against Jared Bernstein and former OECD ambassador Karen Kornbluh — framed as: **"Do we need government to ensure a decent paycheck, or would low-wage workers and the economy be better off without its intervention?"** (Dorn, Roberts, Bernstein & Kornbluh, 2013, [Open to Debate](https://opentodebate.substack.com/p/abolish-minimum-wage)). The franchisee holds the least comfortable seat in that debate: employer of record for the wages, without authority to raise prices.

The sharpest warning here concerns what happens when the system being sold does not exist. GV Wire documented the collapse of Burgerim, which sold franchises across California's Central Valley. The buyers were not investors: **"Most of these franchisees were inexperienced in restaurants or franchising — teachers, cooks, accountants, police officers, engineers, war veterans and real estate professionals."** One Fresno franchisee summarized corporate support in four words: **"They never answered their phone."** The founder **"fled the country after collecting an estimated $57 million in franchise fees,"** and regulators found the company **"concealed the number of cancellations and refunds, mismanaged franchisees initial franchise fees, bounced refund checks, and abandoned its franchise obligations"** (Jakobs, 2021, [GV Wire](https://gvwire.com/2021/03/18/valley-burger-shops-left-holding-the-bag-after-founder-takes-money-runs/)).

Against Jersey Mike's the contrast is precise. Both sold the same promise — a proven system, a recognized brand, corporate support. In one the system existed and compounded into an $8 billion enterprise; in the other the brand was the only asset and the franchise fees *were* the business model. **A prospective franchisee cannot easily tell these apart from outside**, because the disclosure document, the training, and the pitch look similar either way.

Regulation exists to close that gap and is thinner than it looks. Franchise sales require mandatory pre-sale disclosure, and states accept complaints — the New York Attorney General's office maintains a franchise complaint line and points disputants toward mediation, noting that **"Litigation usually signals the end of a business relationship, but the franchise community has increasingly sought the use of mediation to solve franchise disputes"** (Office of the New York Attorney General, [Franchisor-franchisee disputes](https://ag.ny.gov/resources/organizations/franchise-regulation/franchisor-franchisee-disputes)). Note the concession: disclosure requires accurate statements, not a good business, and the recommended remedy is a negotiation between a franchisee who has staked everything and a franchisor who has not.

Anthropic adds a pressure the older franchise literature does not anticipate. Massenkoff and McCrory find that **"Occupations with higher observed exposure are projected by the BLS to grow less through 2034"** (Massenkoff & McCrory, 2026, [Anthropic Research](https://www.anthropic.com/research/labor-market-impacts)). Much of what a franchisor centrally provides — scheduling, inventory forecasting, marketing copy, service scripts — is the standardized cognitive work AI now covers cheaply. If an operator can buy those capabilities directly, the franchisor's contribution narrows toward brand and supply chain while the royalty stays the same share of gross sales.

The most important unanswered question, therefore, is:

**As standardized management becomes cheap to buy, what share of a franchise's value is the brand — and is the royalty still priced correctly?**

The traditional answer bundled everything: you paid a percentage of revenue for the name, the playbook, the purchasing power, and the support, and no one separated them because they came from one source. That bundle is now separable, making the royalty an increasingly explicit payment for brand recognition and supplier terms alone.

The unresolved tension is that standardization is what makes the brand worth anything. A franchisee who deviates locally may serve their market better while eroding the consistency that brought customers in. Every argument for local autonomy is also an argument for why the customer should have gone to an independent restaurant.

The Burgerim franchisees found the limiting case: every constraint of the model, none of its benefits. **The bargain only works if the thing you surrendered your independence for is actually there.** The question before signing is not how much control you give up, but what specifically you receive — and whether existing franchisees, not the franchisor, confirm they received it.
