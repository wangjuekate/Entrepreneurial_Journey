Fifty-Four Hundred to Forty-Six Hundred

The case

Early 2015. Shake Shack has just gone public. The growth is real: 21 Shacks in 2012, 63 by the end of 2014, system-wide sales $81M → $217M. The other table in the same filing is not. Source: FY2014 10-K.

Group Discussion

  • Form groups with classmates who had the same role.
  • Before any discussion of answers, draw the Standardization–Autonomy Dial and its three gates.
  • Say which way your choice moves the dial — toward control and our capital, or speed and theirs — and what it buys.
  • Select one representative to present your group's placement and draw it on the board.
💰

Financial Lead

AUV has fallen $5,367 → $5,017 → $4,611 and Shack-level margin 25.6% → 24.0% while the count went 21 → 63. The filing warns AUVs may not hold outside Manhattan. What test does a new Shack have to pass?

  • A Every site clears our return and payback test on its own before we sign the lease — if that means fewer, fewer.
  • B Company-operate only where the unit test passes; license everywhere else so someone else carries the capital.
  • C Accept the lower average as the price of scale — total profit dollars are still rising.
  • D Open as fast as we can while the brand is hot; the falling average is mix and it'll settle.
💰

Financial Lead — Key Takeaways

  • Apply the test per site, before opening — not as an average afterwards. An average is a report; a gate is a decision.
  • Both tables sit in the same filing. Total sales rising and AUV falling are simultaneously true, and which one a team argues from is a choice about what it wants to believe.
  • (D) is the trap. It uses a true observation to avoid a decision. The filing itself warns that new Shacks “may not be profitable.”
📣

Marketing Lead

The brand was built on a park kiosk, no traditional advertising, and product placement the company is paid for. Same-Shack growth has slowed 7.1% → 4.1%. How do you handle the brand as the footprint leaves New York?

  • A Keep every Shack designed for its own location and community — sameness makes us just another chain.
  • B Build a deliberate national brand now; most people outside New York have never heard of us.
  • C Keep leaning on the free coverage and product placement — it works and costs nothing.
  • D Advertise nationally to push traffic and get same-Shack sales back up.
📣

Marketing Lead — Key Takeaways

  • Hold the standardisation-versus-distinctiveness paradox instead of resolving it by default. Sameness is what makes replication possible and what makes it not worth doing.
  • This is a brand that gets paid for product placement. Buying advertising would spend margin to become more ordinary.
  • (D) is the trap. It treats a 4.1% comparable number as a marketing problem, spends the margin, and converts a destination into a chain.
⚙️

Operation Lead

You operate 31 at home and license 27 internationally. In the Middle East you have “limited and restricted communication” with the licensee's logistics providers, “preventing us from exercising control.” Where should the dial sit?

  • A Operate at home where we control the experience; license only where we genuinely can't operate — and fix supply-chain control in the licence terms.
  • B License harder to grow on someone else's capital, with tighter standards and proper audits.
  • C Operate everything ourselves, including internationally, now we have the money.
  • D Start licensing domestically too — the quickest way to add units without our capital.
⚙️

Operation Lead — Key Takeaways

  • Name the trade: control sold, capital and speed bought. That is the dial, and every option pays for one with the other.
  • The filing already describes the dial failing — “preventing us from exercising control” is the company saying the licence terms did not hold.
  • (D) is the trap. Fastest unit growth, and it hands over the home market where the brand actually lives — at exactly the moment the unit is weakening.
💻

Tech Lead

You cannot see into the Middle East supply chain, you measure success by per-Shack profit and payback, and 32 of 63 Shacks are run by other companies. What do you build?

  • A Reporting that shows supply chain and unit economics in every Shack, including ones we don't own.
  • B Build it properly for our own Shacks first; extend to licensees as contracts renew.
  • C Buy standard restaurant systems off the shelf — not where we should spend engineering effort.
  • D Leave licensees to run their own systems — they're independent operators; that's the point.
💻

Tech Lead — Key Takeaways

  • An unobservable standard is not a standard. Monitorability is the third gate, and more than half the system is run by somebody else.
  • You cannot see into the Middle East supply chain, yet you measure success by per-Shack profit and payback. Those two facts are in direct conflict.
  • (D) is the trap. It restates the failure as a philosophy. This is precisely the position that produced three months of inventory nobody can see.
🔒

Security Lead

27 Shacks are run by other companies, carrying one to three months of inventory, supplied by logistics providers you cannot instruct. What is the top risk and where do you set the bar?

  • A Food safety and supply-chain integrity as one risk across every Shack, licensed or not — a sick customer blames the name on the building.
  • B A proper audit and inspection programme across all licensees before signing another territory.
  • C Standards and indemnities written into the licence agreements, and hold licensees to them.
  • D Licensees run their own restaurants and carry their own food-safety responsibility.
🔒

Security Lead — Key Takeaways

  • Separate who pays the bill from whose brand collapses. Indemnities settle the first and are completely silent on the second.
  • One to three months of perishable inventory sits in a chain you cannot instruct. The exposure is measured in weeks of stock, not in clauses.
  • (D) is the trap. It accurately describes the contract and completely misreads the exposure. The customer saw the name on the building.
🧭

Account Manager

You do not answer a functional question. After all five leads present their selection and a one-sentence defence:

  • 1 Rank the five problems 1–5, each used once, with one sentence justifying #1 that contains a figure from the packet.
  • 2 Weight them — 100 points across the five, in blocks of 10, totalling exactly 100.

Part 3 — Place the company on the dial, and say what a Shack has to be able to do before you are willing to let somebody else run one.

🧭

Account Manager — Key Takeaways

  • Ranking is order; weighting is magnitude. A flat split scores 2 of 6 — the designed penalty for refusing to choose.
  • The strong pattern ranks the dial and unit economics above brand and visibility, keeps food safety off the bottom, and weights the top two at about 70 of 100.
  • Part 3 is answered with the three gates — codifiable, unit-profitable, monitorable. A Shack that fails any one of them is not ready to be handed to somebody else.

Key takeaway

The dial is not a strategy, it is a price. Standardisation buys control and costs speed; autonomy buys capital and costs control. Three gates decide whether you are allowed to turn it: codifiable, unit-profitable, monitorable.

An unobservable standard is not a standard.
