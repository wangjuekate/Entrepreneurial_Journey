Stage 1 — Key takeaways

Combine the evidence before scaling

Franchising: Connect unit economics, ownership and control.

  • Calculate what remains. Operating at home with stronger licence terms (40) and all-Shack reporting (35) leave 25 of the 100 planning units for site-specific design.
  • Apply the payback rule. Operating every Shack costs 60 × 1.5 = 90 because it takes on sites before they clear the payback test. Site-specific design alone leaves 75 for operations and systems.
  • Trace the dependency. The COO’s licence terms need the CTO’s reporting in licensed Shacks. The CMO’s local design work fits the remaining budget; company-only systems leave the control gap open.

Simulation assumptions: planning units, the ceiling, the payback multiplier and the visibility dependency.

Stage 2 — Make Executive Decisions

Chief Financial Officer (CFO)

Average unit volume has fallen from $5,367 thousand to $5,017 to $4,611 over three years, and Shack-level operating profit margin from 25.6% to 24.0%, while the count went from 21 to 63. The filing warns that as you leave Manhattan, “we may not be able to maintain our current average unit volumes.” What test does a new Shack have to pass?

  • A.Company-operate only where the unit test passes, and license everywhere else so somebody else carries the capital.
  • B.Open as fast as we can while the brand is hot; the falling average is just a mix effect and it’ll settle.
  • C.Every site has to clear our return and payback test on its own before we sign the lease — and if that means opening fewer, we open fewer.
  • D.Accept the lower average as the price of scale — total profit dollars are still going up.

Stage 2 — Make Executive Decisions

Chief Operating Officer (COO)

You operate 31 Shacks yourself at home and license 27 internationally. In the Middle East your licensee “delegates the supply function to its own third-party logistics providers… with which we have limited and restricted communication, preventing us from exercising control.” Where should the dial sit?

  • C.Operate everything ourselves, including internationally, now that we have the money to do it.
  • D.License harder to grow faster on somebody else’s capital, but tighten the standards and audit them properly.

Stage 2 — Make Executive Decisions

Chief Technology Officer (CTO)

You have a supply chain in the Middle East you cannot see into, you measure success by “Shack-level operating profit margins, cash-on-cash returns and payback periods”, and 32 of your 63 Shacks are run by other companies. What do you build?

  • A.Build the reporting so we can see supply chain and unit economics in every Shack — including the ones we don’t own. You can’t hold anyone to a standard you can’t see.
  • B.Build it properly for our own Shacks first, then extend it to licensees as contracts come up for renewal.
  • C.Buy standard restaurant systems off the shelf — this isn’t where we should be spending engineering effort.
  • D.Leave licensees to run their own systems — they’re independent operators and that’s the point of licensing.

Stage 2 — Key takeaways

Prove the unit before copying it

Franchising: Make the operating model profitable and monitorable.

  • Require a site-level test. Every site must clear its return and payback test before the lease is signed. A growing Shack count does not establish that the next unit will earn enough.
  • Fund one complete plan. Operate at home and strengthen licence terms (40) + site-specific design (25) + all-Shack reporting (35) = 100 units. IPO proceeds and licensing revenue add no extra units.
  • Make standards observable. License where the company cannot operate, with supply-chain control and reporting that covers every Shack. Local design protects the brand while shared reporting makes standards enforceable.

Simulation assumptions: planning units, the ceiling, the payback multiplier and the visibility dependency.

Stage 3 — CEO: Set Company Priorities

Chief Executive Officer (CEO)

As CEO, rank the four questions from most to least important for this company to still be worth eating at when it has 300 Shacks. Discuss your executives’ recommendations before setting the company’s priorities. Ask each executive: “What is your recommendation, which colleague’s constraint does it depend on, and why is your question important for the company to resolve?” You receive their answers through team chat, not automatic summaries.

  • Unit economics — what a new Shack must earn
  • The brand — staying special while scaling
  • The dial — what we operate vs. what we license
  • Visibility — the systems that let us see every Shack

Use each question once. Why should your first-ranked question take priority? Refer to information two executives shared, say where the company should sit on the dial (what it operates and what it licenses), and name one thing a Shack must be able to do before someone else is allowed to run one.

Stage 3 — Key takeaways

Rank what must work before expansion

Franchising: Explain where the company belongs on the operate–license dial.

  • Put Unit economics and The dial first. Either order fits the template: what each Shack earns and who runs it determine what the company becomes as it expands.
  • Place Visibility third and The brand fourth. Reporting makes licence terms enforceable. Ranking the brand last does not remove the 25-unit design commitment from the plan.
  • State the condition for replication. Use evidence from two executives. Operate at home and license abroad under the scenario’s constraints; require a repeatable, profitable operation whose supply chain and unit economics can be monitored.

These priorities follow the template’s scenario assumptions; explain the dependencies behind your ranking.
