Fifty-Four Hundred to Forty-Six Hundred

The case

Early 2015. Shake Shack has just gone public. The growth is real: 21 Shacks in 2012, 63 by the end of 2014, system-wide sales $81M → $217M. The filing also reports average unit volume and Shack-level margins; assess those alongside total growth. Source: FY2014 10-K.

Group Discussion

  • Form groups with classmates who had the same role.
  • Draw the Standardization–Autonomy Dial and its three gates.
  • Say which way your choice moves the dial — toward control and our capital, or speed and theirs — and what it buys.
  • Cite a figure from your packet, name what your choice gives up, and state one thing that would change your answer.
  • Select one representative to present your group's placement and reasoning on the board.
💰

Financial Lead

AUV has fallen from $5,367 thousand to $4,611 and Shack-level margin from 25.6% to 24.0% while the count went from 21 to 63. The filing warns AUVs may not hold as you leave Manhattan. What test does a new Shack have to pass?

  • A Every site has to clear our return and payback test on its own before we sign the lease — and if that means opening fewer, we open fewer.
  • B Company-operate only where the unit test passes, and license everywhere else so somebody else carries the capital.
  • C Open as fast as we can while the brand is hot; the falling average is just a mix effect and it'll settle.
  • D Accept the lower average as the price of scale — total profit dollars are still going up.
💰

Financial Lead — Key Takeaways

  • System growth and unit performance answer different questions. Explain what each measure reveals and which comparison supports your investment decision.
  • Make assumptions visible. Consider site mix, capital required and operating costs when interpreting averages and projecting a new unit.
  • Connect the test to the dial. Explain how ownership or licensing changes who supplies capital, who receives returns and who bears the consequences if a site underperforms.

Marketing Lead

The brand was built on a kiosk in Madison Square Park, no traditional advertising, and free product placement. Same-Shack sales growth has slowed from 7.1% to 4.1%. How do you handle the brand as the footprint leaves New York?

  • A Keep leaning on the free coverage and product placement — it's worked so far and it costs us nothing.
  • B Start advertising nationally to push traffic and get same-Shack sales back up.
  • C Build a deliberate national brand now, because most people outside New York have never heard of us.
  • D Keep every Shack designed for its own location and community — the moment they all look the same we're just another burger chain.
📣

Marketing Lead — Key Takeaways

  • Distinctiveness and consistency create a tension. Identify what customers should recognize everywhere and what could vary by location under your proposal.
  • Separate diagnosis from promotion. State what evidence would establish why sales growth changed before assessing what your marketing choice could accomplish.
  • Evaluate the effect on replication. Explain how the brand plan affects operators, customers and the cost of opening in a new market.
⚙️

Operation Lead

You operate 31 Shacks yourself at home and license 27 internationally, and in the Middle East you have "limited and restricted communication" with your licensee's logistics providers, "preventing us from exercising control." Where should the dial sit?

  • A Operate them ourselves at home where we control the experience, license only where we genuinely can't operate — and fix the supply-chain control in the licence terms.
  • B Operate everything ourselves, including internationally, now that we have the money to do it.
  • C Start licensing domestically too — it's the quickest way to add units without spending our own capital.
  • D License harder to grow faster on somebody else's capital, but tighten the standards and audit them properly.
⚙️

Operation Lead — Key Takeaways

  • The dial allocates control and capital. Explain what your proposal asks the company and its partners to contribute, and what authority each retains.
  • Apply the three gates to the proposal. Identify the evidence you would seek about codifiability, unit profitability and monitorability before expanding it.
  • Allow for local conditions. Explain whether the same arrangement works across markets and what would justify a different setting.
💻

Tech Lead

You have a supply chain in the Middle East you cannot see into, you measure success by per-Shack profit and payback, and 32 of your 63 Shacks are run by other companies. What do you build?

  • A Leave licensees to run their own systems — they're independent operators and that's the point of licensing.
  • B Buy standard restaurant systems off the shelf — this isn't where we should be spending engineering effort.
  • C Build it properly for our own Shacks first, then extend it to licensees as contracts come up for renewal.
  • D Build the reporting so we can see supply chain and unit economics in every Shack — including the ones we don't own. You can't hold anyone to a standard you can't see.
💻

Tech Lead — Key Takeaways

  • Monitoring should inform action. Specify the operating decision the proposed system supports, who uses its information and how quickly they need it.
  • Compare across different operators. Identify the definitions and access arrangements needed to interpret data consistently across locations.
  • Include implementation costs. Consider partner capability, training and adoption alongside system cost. State how you would know the information is reliable enough to use.
🔒

Security Lead

Twenty-seven Shacks are run by other companies, carrying one to three months of inventory, supplied by logistics providers you have "limited and restricted communication" with. What is the top risk and where do you set the bar?

  • A Write the standards and indemnities into the licence agreements and hold the licensees to them.
  • B Licensees run their own restaurants and carry their own food-safety responsibility — that's what the agreement says.
  • C Treat food safety and supply-chain integrity as one risk across every Shack licensed or not — a customer who gets sick anywhere blames the name on the building.
  • D Put a proper audit and inspection programme across all licensees before we sign another territory.
🔒

Security Lead — Key Takeaways

  • Assess exposure across organizational boundaries. Identify who can prevent harm, who can detect it and who experiences its consequences.
  • Distinguish a standard from evidence of performance. Explain how the team would assess whether its chosen controls work in daily operations.
  • Plan the response as well as the requirement. State who can intervene, how concerns are escalated and what findings would change the pace of expansion.

Account Manager

You do not answer a functional question. After the five leads present, consider the dial, unit economics, food safety and standards, the brand, and visibility.

  • 1 Rank the problems 1–5 by whether replication makes the company stronger or weaker. Use each once. Justify #1 in one sentence containing a figure from your packet.
  • 2 Allocate 100 points across the five problems in blocks of 10, totalling exactly 100.
  • 3 Place the company on the dial. Where should Shake Shack sit between standardization and autonomy, and what must a Shack be able to do before you let someone else run one?

Account Manager — Key Takeaways

  • Priorities need a replication argument. Ranking sets order; weighting expresses relative emphasis. Explain how the choices make the next unit stronger or weaker.
  • Connect the gates to evidence. Describe what you would need to observe before trusting another operator with the model, and where the packet leaves uncertainty.
  • Evaluate the whole arrangement. Explain how the dial setting, brand decisions, unit economics and oversight work together, including what would trigger a change.
