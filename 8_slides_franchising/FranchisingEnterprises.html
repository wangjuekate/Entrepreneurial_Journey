Franchising Enterprises

MENT 2234 · Module 8 · Concept Lecture

Case: Shake Shack · Mental model: the Standardization–Autonomy Dial

Open with the hands-up bit, not with a definition. The gap between the great one and the hundredth one is the whole subject.

Hands up

  • Put your hand up if you have ever had a genuinely great meal somewhere and thought “someone should open a hundred of these.”
  • Now keep your hand up if you have ever been to the hundredth one.
  • Yeah. It was not the same, was it.

That gap has a name

Between the one that was great and the hundredth one that was fine.

The name is franchising, and about half the retail economy you walk past every day is built on solving it.

Actually wait for the hands. The laugh on "it wasn't the same, was it" buys you the next thirty minutes.

What franchising actually is

“You pay me to be allowed to be me, in a place I cannot be.”

What the franchisor gets

Growth without capital. Somebody else buys the building, hires the staff and takes the risk. You collect a royalty on their sales.

What the franchisee gets

A business that already works. No agonising about whether anyone wants this — people already queue for it somewhere else.

So everybody wins and we can all go home early. Except — what did the franchisee actually buy? Not a burger recipe; you can get one of those free. They bought a system: where to put the store, how to lay out the kitchen, what to pay staff, how long a queue may get, which supplier, what temperature, what the sign looks like.

Franchising is not selling a product. It is selling an entire operating manual for a business — and that only works if the business can be written down.

Deliver "everybody wins, go home early" flat, then pause. The reversal is where the real content starts.

The trade nobody names out loud

You sell control. You buy capital and speed.

  • Someone else's money builds your empire.
  • And in exchange, a person you have never met is standing in a kitchen with your name over the door at eleven at night, deciding whether that chicken is still fine.

The trade is not automatically bad

You cannot personally staff a restaurant in Kuwait. You cannot operate inside somebody else's baseball stadium — they own the stadium.

In those cases licensing is not a compromise. It is the only way the thing exists at all.

The mistake is never that companies license. The mistake is licensing before the thing being licensed is ready to be copied.

Land the eleven-o'clock-at-night image slowly. It is the most concrete definition of "losing control" anyone in the room will get.

The mental model — the Standardization–Autonomy Dial

It is not a yes/no question. It is a dial — and you can set it differently in different places.

Beat 2 is the row to stop on: control transfers, reputation does not. Beats 3-5 are the gates, and gate 2 is the one they will skip on Thursday if you rush it.

Read the last row again

Turn the dial toward standardization

Company-operated. You control it. Your capital. Slower growth. Your margin.

Your problem.

Turn it toward autonomy

Licensed or franchised. They control it. Their capital. Fast growth. A royalty.

Still your problem.

Control transfers. Reputation does not.

This is the line that makes franchising a strategy question rather than a legal one. Everything downstream — the gates, the Middle East supply chain — is a consequence of it.

Three gates. Not two.

1 · Codifiable

Can the whole thing be written down?

If not, you are not franchising a system. You are franchising a hope.

2 · Unit-profitable

Does one of them make money before you copy it?

The one people skip, and the one that kills companies. If not, you are about to mass-produce a loss.

3 · Monitorable

Can you see it from a distance?

“It's in the agreement” is not seeing. An annual audit visit is a photograph. What you need is a feed.

A photocopier does not improve the original.

Gate 3 is the one people think they have passed. Push on "through what do you observe it?" until someone says something other than the contract.

Shake Shack, 2014 — the table that made the headlines

  • A company that started as a single hot dog cart in Madison Square Park, run by a restaurateur who genuinely cared about hospitality. It has just gone public.
  • Shacks system-wide: 21 → 40 → 63
  • System-wide sales: $81M → $140M → $217M
  • Total Shack-level profit: $14.2M → $20.4M → $26.9M

Triumph, right?

Tripled the estate. Nearly tripled sales. Profit almost doubled.

Hold that thought for exactly one slide.

Sell this table honestly. If the room is not impressed here, the next slide has nothing to reverse.

Same company. Same three years. Same filing.

Average unit volume

$5,367K → $5,017K → $4,611K

Average weekly sales

$102K → $96K → $89K

Shack-level margin

25.6% → 26.0% → 24.0%

Same-Shack sales growth

7.1% → 5.9% → 4.1%

The company is getting bigger and every individual Shack is getting weaker.

Both tables are true. Both are in the same filing. Only one was in the headlines. And the honest part, in the company's own words: a lot of it is mix — the early Shacks were in Manhattan, and Manhattan is exceptional. The filing says plainly that as they expand “we may not be able to maintain our current average unit volumes.” True. Also not a plan.

Let them find the pattern before you say it. Then give the mix explanation fairly — and then point out that an explanation is not a remedy.

Where they set the dial — and where gate 3 failed anyway

Operate at home, license abroad

Domestic company-operated: 31
Domestic licensed: 5
International licensed: 27

The five domestic licences: two baseball stadiums, a racetrack, and two gates at JFK. Every one is a place Shake Shack physically cannot operate. At the airport, the filing notes the Shacks are “licensed to a third party, though they are managed by Shake Shack employees.” That is a company being extremely careful with gate 3.

And then, from the risk factors

“our international licensed Shacks in the Middle East carry one to three months of inventory… we have had past and ongoing issues ensuring that timely and adequate supplies reach… our licensee delegates the supply function to its own third-party logistics providers… with which we have limited and restricted communication, preventing us from exercising control or instruction.”

Perishable food. Up to three months of inventory. Logistics companies you cannot instruct. In a hot climate.

International licensed AUV over the same three years: $9,665K → $6,077K → $4,588K. The first ones were spectacular. Somebody else chose the newer sites.

The point is not that Shake Shack was careless — they were unusually careful. Gate 3 failed anyway. That is what makes it a gate rather than a checkbox.

The paradox you cannot escape

Standardisation is what makes replication possible. Distinctiveness is what makes it worth doing.

  • Their own filing: “although no two Shacks are alike, we believe that these brand identifiers are critical to the expression of the brand.”
  • Tables made from reclaimed bowling lanes. Each store designed for its own neighbourhood.
  • That is beautiful, it is operationally inconvenient, and it is exactly why the company is worth more than the burger.

Nobody resolves this tension

A chain that is identical everywhere is easy to expand and easy to ignore. A place that is different everywhere is loved and nearly impossible to scale.

You just choose, consciously, where on the line you stand — because if you do not choose, you will drift toward “identical” one completely reasonable decision at a time.

Echo Module 7 deliberately: value destroyed by a series of reasonable decisions. Students should hear the rhyme.

The one line to carry in

Not “how fast can we grow?” — but what has to be true about ONE of them before I am allowed to make another?

Growth is the easy part. There is always someone willing to pay to be you.

Codifiable. Profitable on its own. Visible from a distance. Three gates — and the second one is where companies die, because a photocopier does not improve the original.

You are not scaling a brand. You are scaling whatever the unit currently is.

Last line is the takeaway. Then hand out the role pages.
