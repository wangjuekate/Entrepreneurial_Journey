# The Worst and Best Time to Be a Business Major: Which Half of the Contradiction Are You Training For?

Every business student has heard some version of the same reassurance: the degree is practical, the skills transfer, and there will always be demand for people who understand how organizations make money. The materials from Anthropic, *The New York Times*, and Open to Debate make that reassurance harder to accept at face value. They describe an economy in which the specific tasks a business curriculum has taught for decades are among the tasks most exposed to automation — and, at the same time, an economy in which one person with a laptop can build a company that would once have required a hundred employees. Both things are true at once. The real question is not whether this is a good or bad era to study business, but **which half of that contradiction you are being trained for**.

Start with the pessimistic case, because the evidence for it is unusually specific. Anthropic's Massenkoff and McCrory built a measure they call "observed exposure," combining what models can theoretically do with how people actually use them, and weighting automated uses more heavily than augmentative ones. Their finding is not that unemployment has spiked. It is subtler, and for students more unsettling: **"The averaged estimate in the post-ChatGPT era is a 14% drop in the job finding rate compared to that in 2022 in the exposed occupations"** (Massenkoff & McCrory, 2026, [Anthropic Research](https://www.anthropic.com/research/labor-market-impacts)). The jobs are not vanishing all at once. The doors into them are narrowing. The same study reports that **"Occupations with higher observed exposure are projected by the BLS to grow less through 2034,"** with every ten-percentage-point increase in coverage corresponding to a 0.6-percentage-point drop in projected growth.

Which occupations are those? Not the ones the usual anxiety targets. Massenkoff and McCrory find that **"Workers in the most exposed professions are more likely to be older, female, more educated, and higher-paid"** — a description of credentialed white-collar work, the analysts, accountants, marketers, and coordinators that business schools are built to produce. This is the strongest form of the obsolescence argument: not that human judgment stops mattering, but that the entry-level rungs where judgment used to be *built* are the rungs being removed.

Now the optimistic case, which rests on the same technology. *The New York Times* profiled Matthew Gallagher, who founded the telehealth company Medvi and ran it with more than a dozen AI tools instead of a staff. Medvi generated $401 million in sales in 2025 and was projected to reach $1.8 billion with two employees — Gallagher and his brother (Griffith, 2026, The New York Times, via [GV Wire](https://gvwire.com/2026/04/05/a-1-8-billion-company-with-two-employees-ai-made-it-possible/)). The *Times* also documented how young the new fortunes are: **"Most of the new AI billionaires founded their companies less than three years ago after OpenAI released ChatGPT,"** including a Cursor founding team in their twenties whose company **"was valued at $27 billion in a funding round last month"** (Rocha, 2025, The New York Times, via [GV Wire](https://gvwire.com/2025/12/29/the-new-billionaires-of-the-ai-boom/)).

This is the "one-person company" and the "superindividual." If the marginal cost of producing a competent business document approaches zero, then the scarce input is no longer the person who can produce it — it is the person who knows **which document is worth producing, for whom, and why anyone would pay**. A business education that teaches the second thing is more valuable than it has ever been. A business education that teaches only the first is being commoditized in real time.

Anthropic's usage data suggests the balance may be tipping toward the hopeful reading. In the January 2026 Economic Index, **"The share of conversations classified as augmented jumped 5pp to 52% and the share deemed automated fell 4pp to 45%"** (Appel et al., 2026, [Anthropic Research](https://www.anthropic.com/research/anthropic-economic-index-january-2026-report)).

But augmentation is not automatically a blessing. Reporting on the contractors who generate AI training data — people paid to teach systems to do work like their own — the *Times* quotes historian Margaret O'Mara: **"The machine is hiding the things that humans are doing"** (Kelley, 2026, The New York Times, via [GV Wire](https://gvwire.com/2026/07/11/the-work-of-helping-ai-destroy-work/)). Apparent autonomy can be human labor relocated out of view, and out of the wage structure.

Open to Debate put the question directly to two people who have spent careers on it. In February 2026, the Hopkins Forum in Washington hosted Andrew Yang, founder of the Forward Party, arguing yes against Chris Hughes arguing no, on the motion **"Will AI Make Work Obsolete?"** (Yang & Hughes, 2026, [Open to Debate](https://opentodebate.substack.com/p/andrew-yang-and-chris-hughes-debate)). That the motion is genuinely contested by serious people is itself the finding. Nobody credible is claiming this is settled.

The most important unanswered question, therefore, is:

**If AI removes the entry-level tasks where business judgment has always been learned, where will the next generation of judgment come from?**

This is unresolved in a way the rest is not. We know roughly which tasks are exposed, and we can see solo founders reaching scale that used to require organizations. What no one has shown is how a twenty-two-year-old becomes the person who can direct those tools well, if the apprenticeship of doing the small work first has been automated away.

The optimistic answer is that the apprenticeship moves: students who build and ship real things with AI arrive with judgment the old track never produced. The pessimistic answer is that judgment is not transferable from simulation — that you learn to spot a bad forecast by having built enough bad ones yourself, and that reviewing a model's output teaches far less than producing your own.

Both are plausible, and the difference between them determines whether your degree is the worst investment of the decade or the best. What the readings suggest is that the outcome is not fixed by the technology. It depends on whether you spend the next four years becoming the person who **operates the tools** or the person the tools operate around.
