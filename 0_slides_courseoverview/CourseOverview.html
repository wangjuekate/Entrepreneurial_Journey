The worst and the best time to be a business major

The bad news is specific

Anthropic's labor-market work reports a 14% drop in the job-finding rate in AI-exposed occupations compared with 2022 (Massenkoff & McCrory, 2026).

The most exposed workers are "older, female, more educated, and higher-paid" — a description of credentialed white-collar work. That is what a business school is built to produce.

The good news uses the same technology

Medvi did $401M in sales in 2025 with two employees and a stack of AI tools (Griffith, 2026).

Cursor's founders, in their twenties, were valued at $27B three years after starting (Rocha, 2025).

Both are true at once. The question is not whether this is a good era — it is which half of the contradiction you are training for. (Pre-class reading: WorstBestEraBusinessMajor)

Ask for a show of hands on each side before revealing the second column. The blog is assigned before this class, so they should already have an opinion on record.

Where the value moved

AI drills deep in the narrow tasks the curriculum teaches. Deciding which connection across contexts is worth making takes the whole picture at once — and that is entrepreneurial work.

Beats 1-2: the depth lane. Beat 3: the turn to breadth. Beat 4: the chain. Beat 5: the payoff. Do not click ahead of your own sentence.

So everyone has to become an entrepreneur

  • If a competent business document costs almost nothing to produce, the scarce input is no longer the person who can produce it.
  • It is the person who knows which document is worth producing, for whom, and why anyone would pay.
  • That is the "one-person company" and the "superindividual" — and it is a skill, not a personality type.

What this course is not

It is not career decoration for people who want to start a company someday.

It is training for the half of the contradiction that is still paid: judgment under uncertainty, built by making decisions and living with them.

Head off the objection that only future founders need this. The corporate-entrepreneurship module answers it directly in Week 4.

Three ways to do it — all of them pay

A side hustle to roughly a million, a small private operation you own and run, or a venture-scale startup. Higher ceilings cost more capital and more risk — but no zone on this chart is the “wrong” one.

Beats 2-4 land one zone at a time, smallest first. Ask who is already running a side hustle before beat 2.

Why I chose to teach this set of courses

“He who has a why to live for can bear with almost any how.”

Nietzsche, quoted throughout Viktor Frankl's Man's Search for Meaning (1946) — written after Frankl survived Auschwitz. His argument: meaning is not handed to you and not waiting to be discovered intact. You build it out of the work you take responsibility for.

What I want from this semester

Entrepreneurship is told the same two-sided way: the funding announcement, and the part nobody posts.

I want to see all of you start building your story, starting in this class.

Land the Frankl quote, then hand the frame over to them: this is the pivot from my story to theirs. Pause before moving on to the modules slide.

What you will learn: 8 modules, one rhythm

  • Tuesday — the concept, argued through a real case.
  • Thursday — you run the simulation, then write the reflection.
  • Every module puts one decision in front of you that has to be made on incomplete information.

Why simulations, not lectures

You cannot learn to spot a bad forecast by watching someone else spot one. You learn it by building one, defending it, and being wrong in front of people.

Set the expectation now that Thursday is not a recap day. If they show up unprepared on Thursday it shows immediately in the simulation.

What you should be able to do by December

  • Analyze how social structures, networks and culture shape who gets to be an entrepreneur.
  • Design and evaluate a viable business model with a contemporary framework.
  • Recognize an opportunity, acquire resources for it, and validate it with evidence.
  • Generate solutions through design thinking and creative problem-solving.
  • Assess someone else's venture idea and say why it does or does not work.
  • Decide under uncertainty and resource constraints — and defend the decision.
These are the six syllabus objectives. Every one of them is assessed through the team venture, not through a test.

How to ace this course

60% is a loop you repeat every week. 40% is three team milestones. There is no exam to save you at the end.

Eight beats: walk the loop 1-3, total it at 4, then the milestones 5-6, the total at 7, and only then the make-up path at 8. Do not show beat 8 early or it becomes the takeaway.

The 60%: what "prepared" means

Blog comments — 10%

Before class: 50–100 words with a clear opinion and evidence behind it. "Interesting post" is not a comment.

In-class participation — 20%

Be a decision-maker in the simulation, not a spectator. Use evidence when you defend a recommendation.

Simulation reflections — 30%

After class: what you did, what it cost, what you would do differently next time. Specific beats polished.

Read one strong and one weak blog comment aloud in Week 2. Setting the bar by example saves a semester of arguing about grades.

The 40%: your team's venture

  • Teams of 4–5, developing one new venture concept all semester.
  • Team register — 5%, Week 1.
  • Midterm pitch video + report — 15%, Week 7.
  • Final pitch video + report — 20%, Week 15 and finals week.

The through-line

Each module hands your team a tool. The midterm is the first honest test of whether the idea survives contact with those tools; the final is whether you fixed what broke.

Team formation happens Thursday of Week 1 — point them at the TeamFormationResources deck.

If you have to miss a week

The make-up path

Up to 3 class exemptions. To make one up: interview a classmate about what happened in the simulation and submit a reflection based on that interview.

You still owe the reflection. You just source it from someone who was in the room.

  • More than 3 unexcused absences reduces the participation grade.
  • Late work: −10% per day; nothing accepted more than a week late.
  • Tell me in advance when you know you will be out.
Being explicit about the make-up path up front cuts the volume of end-of-semester email dramatically.
