The Sweets & the Bitterness of Entrepreneurship

MENT 2234 · Module 1 · Concept Lecture

Case: WeWork · Mental model: the Risk–Return Asymmetry Map

Tell this like a stand-up set. Pause where the script says pause, let them laugh, then land the point. Do not open with the risks.

It is not a straight line up

  • Everybody thinks entrepreneurship is a line going up and to the right.
  • It is a roller coaster — and the tickets are non-refundable.
  • Same ride, same people. Some get off at the top. Some get off at the bottom.

Today I sell you the ride twice

First why people get on. Then why some of them get off wishing they had never seen the amusement park.

Stay with me — the second half is the half that is usually left out.

Say "non-refundable" and stop. That is the line the whole hour hangs on. Ask for a show of hands: who here wants to start something?

A true story — the sweets

  • At 27, broke and sleeping on a friend's couch, two founders rented out air mattresses in their own apartment during a design conference. Three mattresses. Breakfast included.
  • To fund it they printed novelty cereal boxes and sold them at $40 a box. Grown men selling breakfast cereal to survive.
  • Ten years on, that couch-surfing guy is a billionaire and the company is worth more than the entire Hilton chain — a chain that owns actual buildings.

He owns nobody's building

He owns the app that owns the idea. That is Airbnb.

Remember the cereal box. It comes back in twenty minutes, on a private jet.

Pause after "cereal". Let them laugh. The cereal box is a callback you will cash in on the WeWork slide — plant it deliberately.

What are the returns?

Financial freedom

Not "rich" — free. The kind where the alarm clock becomes optional.

A salary has a number written on it. A company you own does not.

Fulfillment

The first time a total stranger pays for a thing that did not exist until you made it exist.

A teacher grades your work. A boss approves it. A customer votes with their wallet. That vote hits different.

To be remembered

Walt Disney was rejected from a newspaper job — the editor said he "lacked imagination."

That editor's name is a trivia question. Most people rent their years; founders try to buy a monument.

Freedom, meaning, immortality. Deliver it as a pitch — "sign here" — then turn the lights down on the next slide.

The flip side — the $47 billion man

  • Adam Neumann's WeWork was "worth" $47B. He flew a private jet and — allegedly — smuggled weed across a border inside a cereal box.
  • He rang the bell early. He threw the party. He told everyone the party would never end.
  • Then the company tried to go public. The moment strangers could open the books — the same books you will open — $47B became about $7B in weeks.

The twist that breaks your brain

He did not go to debtors' prison. He walked away with a parachute worth around $1 billion.

So was that a happy ending? Ask the 2,000 employees who lost their jobs and their stock.

Cash the cereal-box callback here — entrepreneurship runs on cereal. Then stop performing. The $1B-versus-2,000-people contrast is the pivot of the lecture.

What are the risks? Not one risk — four

Financial

The loan. The personal guarantee with your name on it. Founders sign these all the time.

Career

The three years you did not spend getting promoted somewhere safe.

Family

The birthdays. The people who signed up for a person and got a company.

Mental

The 3 a.m. ceiling. No off switch; the phone is a leash.

These four are the horizontal axis of the model two slides from now. Every business textbook gets lazy here and calls it "risk."

Land these in two clicks, two at a time. Say explicitly that this is the axis they are about to draw — it stops the figure feeling like a new topic.

The bitterness, up close

Deep in debt

The scoreboard does not care about your vision. WeWork lost $4.6B in a single year and spent more running its buildings than it earned from them.

Sign a personal guarantee and the failure is not abstract. It follows you home.

The slaved schedule

Musk slept on the Tesla factory floor during production hell. Romantic in a keynote.

Now live it: the business follows you into the shower, into dinner, into your kid's birthday party.

Regret

The founder who sold too early and watched the buyer make the billions. The founder who held on too long and rode a good company into the ground.

Both of them lie awake.

Being your own boss sounds like freedom until you realise you hired the worst boss you have ever had — one who never lets you clock out, because he is you.

Change tone here. Stop selling and start levelling with them. The "worst boss" line works only if you have dropped the performance first.

The mental model — the Risk–Return Asymmetry Map

Everything in the last twenty minutes fits on two axes — and the two axes are not symmetric. That asymmetry is what makes this a model and not a chart.

Make them draw it. Beat 1 the axes — say out loud that the horizontal is four risks stacked. Beats 2-4 the three points, A first. Beat 5 is the whole lecture. Do not click to beat 6 until someone has tried to answer it.

How to read the map

Point A — high risk, low return

Most ventures live here. This is not failure yet; it is the middle of the ride.

WeWork in 2021 is here, and that is the moment you walk into on Thursday.

Point B — low risk, high return

Everyone thinks they are aiming here. Almost nobody lands here.

The ones who do usually had a structural advantage they rarely admit to.

Point C — high risk, high return

The founder who got off at the top.

Same curve as A. Different point on the risk axis, and a different exit.

Ask the room to place a company they know on the map before you reveal each card. Getting B wrong is the useful mistake — most students put their own idea there.

The asymmetry — the part that makes it a model

Your upside is capped by your ambition. Your downside is capped by your signature.

Return has no ceiling

A company you own has no number written on it. You cannot gain more than you built — but nothing caps what you can build.

Risk has a floor you guaranteed

You can lose more than you invested. Neumann and his 2,000 employees rode the same curve — they just sat at different points on the risk axis. He had a parachute. They had stock.

This is the slide to photograph. If they remember one sentence from Module 1, make it the quote.

The one question the model makes you ask

Not “how big is the win?” — but which axis does this move me on, and can I survive the move if I am wrong?

Run it on a real decision

Expanding into a building that costs $1.20 to run for every $1.00 it earns moves you right on the risk axis and barely at all up on the return axis.

That is not ambition. That is paying more for the same seat on the ride.

Do this one out loud with them, slowly. The $1.20-for-$1.00 building is a real WeWork number and it reappears in the simulation packet — they should recognise it.

The one line to carry in

The sweet and the bitter are not two rides. They are the same ride.

The freedom and the debt come off the same decisions. The fulfillment and the sleepless nights are the same nights. Nobody sells you a ticket to just the top of the roller coaster.

The difference between the founder who gets off at the top and the one who gets off at the bottom is not how they feel. Everybody feels the drop. The difference is what they do at the bottom of it.

Emotion is the weather. The numbers are the map. Neumann felt like a genius at $47B and never looked at the numbers. Airbnb's founders felt like failures eating cereal and looked every single day.

Long pause before the last line. Then hand out the role pages — do not let the emotion of the slide be the last thing in the room.

Next: the simulation

  • You will open WeWork's real FY2021 books at the exact moment the ride hit the top.
  • Each of you holds one role and one decision, with four options and not enough information.
  • Before you choose, put your decision on the map. Say out loud which axis it moves you on.

Your job is not to feel about it

Your job is to decide — and then to defend the decision with a number rather than a feeling.
