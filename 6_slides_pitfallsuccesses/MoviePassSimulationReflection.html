MoviePass at 197%

The case

Spring 2018. Helios and Matheson Analytics owns a majority of MoviePass. In August 2017 the price was cut to $9.95 a month for a movie a day; within four months there were over a million paying subscribers. Growth like that normally means you have won. Source: FY2017 10-K.

Group Discussion

  • Form groups with classmates who had the same role.
  • Before any discussion of answers, draw the Decision Pattern grid — reversibility against consequence.
  • Put your decision in a box: one-way door or two-way door? Then decide at the speed that box allows.
  • Select one representative to present your group's placement and draw it on the board.
💰

Financial Lead

You have $24.9 million in the bank, the company burned $27.4 million running itself last year, and cost of revenues is 197% of revenue. Where does the next dollar go?

  • A Fix what a subscription costs and what it earns before spending another dollar on acquisition.
  • B Raise capital now while the story is hot, and use the time to fix the economics.
  • C Cut operating expenses hard — $45.9 M of them against $10.4 M of revenue.
  • D Keep buying subscribers; at enough scale the theaters have to offer a better deal.
💰

Financial Lead — Key Takeaways

  • Reason from the 197% ratio, not from the subscriber count. About $2.00 goes out for every $1.00 that comes in.
  • $24.9 million in the bank against a $27.4 million annual burn is less than a year — and the gap widens with every subscriber added.
  • (D) is the trap. This is the actual strategy, and it is why the company died. Scale gives leverage only if you survive long enough to use it.
📣

Marketing Lead

A million people signed up in four months — and every one of them loses money at $9.95. What do you do with the marketing budget?

  • A Stop paying to acquire loss-making subscribers; move to a price that covers the cost to serve.
  • B Split the plans — cheap tier for light users, higher price for heavy users.
  • C Keep growing but push annual prepay, to hold the cash up front.
  • D Push $9.95 harder — the subscriber number is the story; go for the next milestone.
📣

Marketing Lead — Key Takeaways

  • Separate growth in subscribers from growth in value. A million sign-ups in four months proved demand at that price, which was never the thing in doubt.
  • Annual prepay moves cash forward without changing the economics — it buys time and enlarges the liability at the same time.
  • (D) is the trap. Optimising the metric that is causing the loss. The subscriber count was the headline, and chasing headlines is what this module is about.
⚙️

Operation Lead

Cost of revenues rose 323% because MoviePass buys most tickets at full retail and hands them to subscribers. What do you do about the cost of the product itself?

  • A Renegotiate ticket economics with the theater chains before adding anyone else.
  • B Cap how many movies a subscriber can see, to bound the worst case per member.
  • C Concentrate on cities where partner deals already exist; stop serving the rest.
  • D Keep paying full price — at enough audience share the theaters will come to us.
⚙️

Operation Lead — Key Takeaways

  • Scale multiplies a per-unit loss. Cost of revenues rose 323% because the company buys the product at retail and sells it below cost.
  • Capping usage bounds the worst case per member — the only lever here that does not require a counterparty to agree to anything.
  • (D) is the trap. A reseller paying full retail has no margin to scale into. Volume multiplies a per-unit loss; it does not dilute it.
💻

Tech Lead

The app handles sign-up, theater check-in, and the data the company keeps calling its real asset. Cash is short. What do you build and what do you buy?

  • A Build real-time visibility into what each subscriber costs — right now nobody can see it.
  • B Build the ticketing integrations with theaters, because those cut the cost per ticket.
  • C Buy analytics off the shelf to get numbers this month instead of next quarter.
  • D Ship consumer features that drive sign-ups; instrument the economics later.
💻

Tech Lead — Key Takeaways

  • An invisible unit cost is why the price decision was made blind. Nobody in the company could see what a subscriber cost in real time.
  • The data the company kept calling its real asset was never instrumented for the one number that decided whether it survived.
  • (D) is the trap. “Instrument later” is how you end up at 197% without noticing.
🔒

Security Lead

Subscribers hand over a payment card and check in from their phone at the theater — so you hold card details and location history, while the auditors are questioning whether the company survives. Where do you set the bar?

  • A Full investment now — you hold card details for a million people.
  • B Risk-based: payment cards and location history first; lower-severity controls wait.
  • C Push the obligation onto the third-party suppliers who run the systems, via contracts.
  • D Defer until the company is making money; live with the exposure until then.
🔒

Security Lead — Key Takeaways

  • Classify by severity and put payment card plus location history in the top tier. Together they are a far more sensitive record than either alone.
  • Contracts move the bill, not the breach — and the auditors are already questioning whether the company survives.
  • (D) is the trap. A card-plus-location breach at a company already under a going-concern warning is not survivable.
🧭

Account Manager

You do not answer a functional question. After all five leads present their selection and a one-sentence defence:

  • 1 Rank the five problems 1–5, each used once, with one sentence justifying #1 that contains a figure from the packet.
  • 2 Weight them — 100 points across the five, in blocks of 10, totalling exactly 100.

Part 3 — Which of these five is a one-way door? Name it out loud and say what makes it irreversible.

🧭

Account Manager — Key Takeaways

  • Ranking is order; weighting is magnitude. A flat split scores 2 of 6 — the designed penalty for refusing to choose.
  • The strong pattern ranks cash and ticket cost above growth, weighting those two at about 70 of 100 between them.
  • The $9.95 price cut is the one-way door. You can raise the number; you cannot unmake the expectation it created in a million people.

Key takeaway

Ventures rarely die of one bad decision. They die of a pattern — the same speed applied to every door in the building. Decide two-way doors fast and one-way doors slowly, and never confuse which is which.

Volume multiplies a per-unit loss. It does not dilute it.
