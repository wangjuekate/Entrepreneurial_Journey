Common Pitfalls and Successes

MENT 2234 · Module 6 · Concept Lecture

Case: MoviePass / Helios and Matheson · Mental model: the Decision Pattern grid

Outcomes come from patterns of decisions, not single events. Say that in the first minute so nobody spends the hour hunting for the one fatal mistake.

The student club's big choice

  • An entrepreneurship club gets the chance to host a famous founder on campus. Large budget, venue booked months ahead, the club's reputation on the line.
  • Some members want several weeks of information-gathering and stakeholder consultation before deciding.
  • At the same time, a second decision: whether to change the design of the event poster.
  • The club runs the same lengthy process for both. They miss the deadline to secure the speaker, and spend three weeks on the poster.

Four questions

Were both decisions equally important?

Should they have used the same process?

What happens when an organization treats every decision as carrying the same risk?

Have you seen a group get slow by overanalyzing small things?

The last question always lands. Take two or three real answers — group projects, student government, a job. It makes the grid feel like a diagnosis rather than a framework.

Not all decisions are the same

Type 1 — one-way doors

Difficult or impossible to reverse. High impact. Long-term consequences.

Acquiring a company · launching a degree program · entering a new market · building a factory

Approach: gather substantial information, consult stakeholders, analyze carefully, move deliberately.

Type 2 — two-way doors

Reversible. Experimental. Lower risk.

Changing a website design · testing a campaign · adjusting a classroom activity · piloting a process

Approach: decide quickly, experiment, learn from the result, change direction if you need to.

Ask yourself the obvious follow-up: why do organizations so reliably treat Type 2 decisions as if they were Type 1?

Get examples of each from their own experience before revealing the boxes. The disagreements about which is which are exactly the skill being taught.

The mental model — the Decision Pattern grid

Two axes: can I walk this back, and what does it cost me if I cannot. Two answers, four boxes, and a rule for each box.

Make them draw it. Beats 2-5 one box at a time. Beat 6 is the pair of failure modes — note they are opposites. Hold beat 7 until someone in the room has guessed which box the price cut was in.

Two ways to get it wrong, and they are opposites

Treating a two-way door like a one-way door

The club spending three weeks on a poster. Slow, cautious, and expensive in opportunities missed.

This is analysis paralysis, consensus obsession, and one-size-fits-all process.

Treating a one-way door like a two-way door

Moving fast and cheerfully through a door that locks behind you.

This is the expensive one. This is MoviePass.

Notice that the standard advice — “be decisive” or “do your homework” — is right half the time and catastrophic the other half, and the advice never tells you which half you are in. The grid does.

This is why the model beats the slogan. If a student can only remember one slide from this module, it should be this one.

How much information is enough?

  • Most decisions should be made with roughly 70% of the information you wish you had.
  • Waiting for 90% costs you: lost opportunities, slow response, reduced innovation, competitors moving first.
  • But 70% of what? The grid answers that. In a two-way door, 70% is generous. In the bottom-right box, 70% may be reckless.

Run it live

A startup has an AI study tool. Testing is promising. Some technical issues are unresolved. Competitors are launching. Three more months would produce more data.

A: wait three months. B: launch now and improve on feedback.

Which information is essential, and which is merely desirable? When does caution become costly?

Split the room and vote before discussing. Then ask which box the launch decision is in — that reframing usually flips several votes.

Disagree and commit

  • A product team debates a new feature. Half think it is valuable, half think it is too risky. After extensive discussion, no consensus emerges.
  • Traditional outcome: the debate continues, more meetings happen, decision velocity collapses.
  • The alternative is one sentence: “I disagree with this decision, but I commit to supporting it.”

What it buys you

Honest debate preserved. Endless meetings avoided. Movement despite uncertainty. Learning through action rather than argument.

Ask: what would happen if every decision required unanimous agreement?

The limit case: sometimes a disagreement is not a difference of opinion but a difference of goals — marketing wants growth, compliance wants risk reduction, and neither accepts the other's assumptions. More meetings will not fix that. That one escalates.

Draw out the difference between healthy disagreement and genuine misalignment. Teams that never escalate look harmonious right up until they are not.

Three tools, and the grid decides which one you may reach for

The grid

Tells you which box a decision is in — and therefore which of the other two tools you are allowed to use.

The 70% rule

Tells you when you have enough information to stop gathering.

Disagree and commit

Tells you how to stop debating without pretending you agree.

Reach for speed in the bottom-right box and you get MoviePass. Reach for deliberation in the top-left and you get three weeks on a poster.

This ordering is the thing students most often get backwards. The grid comes first; it is not one tool among three.

The case: a price is a one-way door

  • August 2017. MoviePass cuts its price to $9.95 a month — unlimited movies. A million people sign up in four months.
  • It looked like the best two-way door in the world: “it's just a price, we can change it back.”
  • You cannot change it back. Once a million people have anchored on $9.95, raising the price does not return you to where you started — it returns you to a smaller, angrier company.

What the filing shows

Cost of delivering the service: 197% of revenue — about $2.00 spent for every $1.00 collected.

The year before the price cut, that same ratio was 72%, and the business made a gross profit.

The decision was fast. The door was one-way. The consequence was the company.

Let them argue about whether a price cut is really irreversible before you give the answer. The anchoring argument is the one that convinces — not the accounting.

The one question the model makes you ask

Before any decision: if this is wrong, can I walk it back — and what does it cost me if I cannot?

That is it. Two answers, four boxes, and a rule for each box.

The 70% rule tells you when you have enough information. Disagree and commit tells you how to stop debating. But the grid tells you which of those tools you are even allowed to reach for.

Have them run the question on a decision their own team venture faces this week. Most teams find they are being slow about something reversible.

The one line to carry in

Ventures do not usually die of one bad decision. They die of a pattern — the same process applied to every door in the building.

The failure modes to watch for in your own team: decisions made by whoever argues longest rather than whoever has the evidence; the quietest person in the room being right and nobody finding out; and confusing being tired of a debate with having resolved it.

Name the three team failure modes slowly. Students recognise their own group projects in all three, which is exactly the point.

Next: the simulation

  • You will open Helios and Matheson's real annual report for 2017 — the year of the price cut.
  • Each of you gets one decision and four options.
  • Before you choose, put your decision in the grid. Say out loud which box it is in — then decide at the speed that box allows.

For the Account Manager
