Common Pitfalls and Successes

MENT 2234 · Module 6 · Concept Lecture

How do you decide when to move quickly and when to investigate?

Case: MoviePass / Helios and Matheson

Start with the tension: both delay and premature commitment can be costly. The goal is to match the decision process to the decision, then use that match repeatedly. Do not reveal the grid yet.

The student club's big choice

  • A club must decide by Friday whether to sign a nonrefundable speaker contract that uses most of its annual budget.
  • It must also choose between two designs for a digital event poster.
  • The team requests three weeks of research and unanimous approval for both decisions.
  • The speaker deadline passes. The poster is still under discussion.

What went wrong?

Did both decisions need the same evidence, discussion, and approval?

Would “just decide faster” solve both problems?

What makes these decisions different?

Take answers before naming the dimensions. Separate difficulty of undoing the choice from damage if it goes wrong. The contract needs focused review before Friday, not unlimited deliberation.

First: can you undo the commitment?

Two-way door

You can reverse the choice at an acceptable cost.

The digital poster: replace the design if it does not work.

Ask: how quickly can we undo it, and what remains afterward?

One-way door

Reversal is impossible or costly.

The signed contract: canceling does not recover the nonrefundable payment.

Ask: which money, obligations, or relationships stay committed?

Classify the specific commitment, not the whole project. Exploring a speaker is different from signing the contract.

Reversibility is a matter of degree. One-way and two-way doors are shorthand, not labels for entire industries or projects. A reversible action can still cause damage before it is stopped.

Second: what happens if you are wrong?

Reversible can still be costly

A club can remove an inaccurate sponsor claim from its website quickly.

But people may already have relied on it. The page is reversible; the resulting harm may not be.

Check exposure before publishing.

Permanent can still be minor

A small batch of inexpensive posters cannot be unprinted.

If the design disappoints, the loss is limited and affordable.

A brief check may be enough.

Ask separately: how hard is reversal? And how large is the downside, including harm before reversal?

This is the necessary second layer. Ask students to compare the two examples: which is easier to undo, and which could do more damage? Do not equate reversible with harmless or permanent with catastrophic.

Two pitfalls: too much process, too little protection

Overprocessing a small choice

Three weeks on the digital poster adds little protection when a quick trial and revision would work.

Cost: time, attention, and opportunities lost.

Underchecking a major commitment

Signing the speaker contract without checking the budget or cancellation terms puts scarce resources at risk.

Cost: losses the team cannot easily recover.

Success means proportionate review within the decision deadline. Neither speed nor caution is always the right answer.

Return to the opening story. Ask what the club should do before Friday: delegate the poster, review contract terms and budget with the right people, then decide. Avoid suggesting that important decisions should simply take longer.

How much information is enough?

Resolve what could change the choice

For the speaker: can we afford the downside, what are the terms, and what evidence supports attendance?

For the poster: are the details accurate, and can we replace it?

Missing information matters according to what it could change.

Set a stopping rule

Before gathering more data, name the uncertainty, the evidence needed, and the deadline.

Investigate unknowns that could make the commitment unacceptable.

Learn the remaining details through a bounded test when possible.

Treat “70% of the information” as a reminder that certainty is unavailable, not a measurable threshold or permission to skip essential checks.

Connect evidence requirements to the two dimensions already established. Ask: what finding would make you decline the contract? If another week of research cannot change the choice, what is it buying? The percentage is a heuristic, not an evidence standard.

You can redesign the decision before making it

A broad launch

An AI study tool has promising early tests and unresolved reliability questions.

Launching to every student exposes many users before the team understands the errors.

Waiting or launching at full scale are not the only choices.

A bounded pilot

Invite a small group, explain the limits, cap the time and spending, and define when to stop.

Resolve essential data-handling issues before the pilot; track errors and learning value during it.

Expand only when the evidence supports expansion.

A smaller commitment can reduce exposure and make reversal easier. Reassess when the scale changes.

Ask students to name what the pilot can teach and what it cannot establish. A small test is not automatically safe, and success with a few volunteers does not prove readiness for everyone. This is the bridge from diagnosis to designing a better decision.

Then: disagree and commit

Before the decision

Agree on the goal, constraints, and who owns the decision.

Invite evidence from every role, including objections that could change the plan.

Resolve critical unknowns; escalate conflicts over goals or authority.

After the decision

When reasonable disagreement remains, the owner decides and the team supports execution.

Set a review date and a signal that would trigger a change.

Commit to the agreed test, while continuing to report contrary evidence.

Consensus is not required for every choice. Commitment does not mean suppressing evidence or continuing after a stop condition is met.

This tool follows classification and evidence gathering. It resolves remaining disagreement; it does not substitute for essential checks. Ask: what would you need to know before you could support a choice you opposed?

Now assemble the decision process

1. Classify

What exactly are we committing to? How hard is reversal, and how large is the downside?

2. Prepare

What evidence is essential? Can a smaller test reduce exposure? Who decides, and by when?

3. Act and review

Make the choice, support execution, and monitor the agreed signals. Reassess before expanding.

The Decision Pattern grid now summarizes the first step and guides the next two. It is a starting diagnosis, not an automatic answer.

Ask students to draw two axes from memory before showing the next slide. They have already reasoned through both dimensions, evidence requirements, and team execution. The grid should now compress familiar reasoning rather than introduce unexplained rules.

The mental model — the Decision Pattern grid

Two independent questions: how hard is it to undo? And how much harm if wrong? Match the process, then revisit as exposure changes.

Reveal the axes first and have students predict each response before beats 2–5. Beat 6 reconnects to the club. Beat 7 asks students to apply the model to MoviePass without supplying a verdict. Reversal difficulty and downside are continua; the boxes guide judgment. No box eliminates the need for a deadline or basic checks.

The mental model to carry forward

Match the process to the commitment.
Check reversibility and downside.
Act, learn, and reassess.

A pitfall becomes a pattern when a team repeatedly overprocesses small choices or underchecks major commitments. A better pattern repeatedly matches evidence, ownership, and review to the decision.

Ask students to explain why neither always move fast nor always gather more information is sufficient. Judge decision quality by the evidence and process available at the time, not just whether the outcome happened to be favorable.

For the Account Manager

When ranking the four problems, compare downside, reversal difficulty, urgency, and dependencies.

Which needs attention first, and why? Do not assume the largest number or a particular box automatically sets the order.

Keep the exercise diagnostic rather than answer-giving. Ask for reasoning tied to the filing. Students may disagree about a placement if they define the commitment differently; make those definitions explicit before comparing choices.
