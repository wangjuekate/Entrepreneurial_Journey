TOMS at 20: Mission and Margin

The case

The role packets use TOMS' 2025 Impact Report, presented as its twentieth-anniversary edition. The company reports $200 million in shoe donations and cash grants and more than 106 million lives impacted cumulatively. The case examines its shift from One-for-One to grants. Treat these as company-reported figures and distinguish the claims from the evidence supplied to support them.

Group Discussion

  • Form groups with classmates who had the same role.
  • Draw the Mission–Margin Axis — community welfare on one end, profit on the other.
  • Say whether your choice makes mission and margin reinforce each other or work against each other.
  • Cite a figure or phrase from your packet, name what your choice gives up, and state one thing that would change your answer.
  • Select one representative to present your group's placement and reasoning on the board.
💰

Financial Lead

Twenty years and $200 million given. But the original promise was a fixed cost per unit, and it is gone — giving is now grants, decided each year. A bad trading year is coming eventually. Where does the giving money come from?

  • A A published share of profit — it scales with the business, and a bad year means a smaller number, not a broken promise.
  • B The marketing budget — honestly, it's what makes people choose us.
  • C An endowment in a separate foundation, insulated from how trading goes.
  • D Whatever's left after a good year, and nothing in a bad one.
💰

Financial Lead — Key Takeaways

  • Trace the link between mission and margin. Explain how your funding choice connects business performance to social spending, and who decides when that spending changes.
  • Test the structure in a bad year. The lecture asks what happens when the company loses money. Compare the consequences for beneficiaries, partners and the business under your proposal.
  • State what the numbers can support. Cumulative giving and an annual commitment answer different questions. Identify the financial information you would need to assess whether your plan can last.
📣

Marketing Lead

"You buy a pair, a child gets a pair" was the clearest promise in retail. What replaced it is more useful and far harder to put on a shoebox. How do you describe the company now?

  • A Lead with the biggest cumulative numbers — 106 million lives, $200 million given — and don't draw attention to the change.
  • B Go back to one-for-one — customers understood it, and a promise people understand is worth more than one they don't.
  • C Keep one simple headline number, but publish the outcome evidence underneath it for anyone who looks.
  • D Report what actually changed for people, verified by someone outside the company — and accept it's a harder story to tell.
📣

Marketing Lead — Key Takeaways

  • Clarity and accuracy can create tension. Connect your message to the lecture's honesty tradeoff: explain what customers would understand and what a short story might leave out.
  • Match the claim to the evidence. Specify the period, meaning and limits of any number you use. Explain what the evidence supports about activity, reach or change in people's lives.
  • Trace the feedback loop. Describe how the message might affect sales, trust and support for the mission. State what would show that those effects reinforce one another.

Operation Lead

Giving is now spread across mental health, gun violence, education and disaster relief, through a large roster of partners. Every cause is real. The money is finite. How do you structure the portfolio?

  • A Back fewer partners with bigger, multi-year money, and hold them to measured outcomes.
  • B Keep the breadth, but make every partner report against the same handful of outcome measures.
  • C Spread it as widely as possible — the more causes and countries we can name, the bigger the story.
  • D Let local partners set their own priorities and fund them flexibly — they know their communities better than we do.
⚙️

Operation Lead — Key Takeaways

  • Breadth and depth serve different goals. The lecture asks how expanding reach affects support for each community. Explain who benefits from your allocation and what your choice leaves unfunded.
  • Consider whose priorities guide the work. Identify how the company, partners and beneficiaries would influence funding decisions and how you would handle disagreement.
  • Judge the portfolio over time. Explain what evidence would show progress, how results across different causes would be interpreted, and when you would revise the allocation.

Tech Lead

The report counts dollars given and lives "impacted". Nobody outside the company audits either number, and "impacted" is not defined anywhere. What do you build?

  • A Outcome measurement with our partners, checked by someone who doesn't work for us.
  • B Fund our partners' own measurement capacity instead of imposing a system — they keep it after our grant ends.
  • C Keep counting what we give — it's cheap, it's honest about the input, and it's the number customers respond to.
  • D Buy an off-the-shelf impact platform so we get numbers this year rather than in three.
💻

Tech Lead — Key Takeaways

  • Define the question before the metric. The lecture distinguishes resources committed from changes experienced. State what your system would measure and what it would still leave unknown.
  • Measurement has an opportunity cost. Compare the learning your proposal could provide with its cost, time and demands on partners. Explain how that learning would change a decision.
  • Coordinate proof with care. Discuss the plan with the Security Lead. Identify what each data item contributes to the analysis and what burden or exposure it creates for beneficiaries.
🔒

Security Lead

Proving outcomes means collecting data about beneficiaries — some in mental-health crisis, some in violence-prevention programmes. A leak would not embarrass the company; it could endanger them. How much do you collect?

  • A Collect as much detail as we can get — the richer the data, the better we can prove the impact is real.
  • B Leave the data with the partners and write the obligations into the grant agreements.
  • C Run a full protection and audit programme across our partners before any beneficiary data moves.
  • D Collect the least we can and keep it de-identified — for these programmes a leak doesn't embarrass us, it endangers them.
🔒

Security Lead — Key Takeaways

  • Beneficiaries are stakeholders in measurement. Assess the proposal from the perspective of people whose experiences become data. Explain how their interests shape your decision.
  • Evidence and protection must be considered together. Identify the question the team needs to answer, the information it requires and the possible consequences of collecting or sharing it.
  • Responsibilities must survive the grant. Explain who manages access, retention and incidents across partners, what resources they need, and what happens when funding ends.

Account Manager

You do not answer a functional question. After the five leads present, consider funding, measurement, beneficiary data, the story, and partners.

  • 1 Rank the problems 1–5 by what determines whether this is still a social enterprise in twenty years. Use each once. Justify #1 in one sentence containing a figure from your packet.
  • 2 Allocate 100 points across the five problems in blocks of 10, totalling exactly 100.
  • 3 Place TOMS on the Mission–Margin Axis: nonprofit → social enterprise → corporation. Explain where it sits today and whether mission and margin reinforce or work against each other.
🧭

Account Manager — Key Takeaways

  • Placement and structure are separate judgments. Explain where TOMS sits on the Mission–Margin Axis, then trace how the team's choices make the two goals reinforce or compete with each other.
  • Priorities reveal tradeoffs. Ranking states order; weighting states relative emphasis. Defend both against the twenty-year horizon and identify whose interests bear the costs.
  • Evaluate the decisions together. Funding, evidence, communication, partners and beneficiary protection interact. Explain how your plan would respond to a bad trading year or disappointing social results.

Key takeaway

Use the axis to locate the company, then examine the relationship between its goals. Trace how business activity supports the mission, how the mission affects the business, and where tradeoffs arise.
