TOMS at 20: Mission and Margin

The case

2025. TOMS is twenty years old: $200 million given away, 106 million lives it says it has impacted, and a B Corp certification. It also no longer does the thing it is famous for — One-for-One is over. Source: the company's own impact report. Nobody audits an impact report; that is part of the lesson.

Group Discussion

  • Form groups with classmates who had the same role.
  • Before any discussion of answers, draw the Mission–Margin Axis — community welfare on one end, profit on the other.
  • Say whether your choice makes mission and margin reinforce each other or work against each other.
  • Select one representative to present your group's placement and draw it on the board.
💰

Financial Lead

Twenty years and $200 million given — but the original promise was a fixed cost per unit, and it is gone. Giving is now grants, decided each year. A bad trading year is coming eventually. Where does the giving money come from?

  • A A published share of profit — scales with the business; a bad year means a smaller number, not a broken promise.
  • B An endowment in a separate foundation, insulated from trading.
  • C The marketing budget — it is what makes people choose us.
  • D Whatever is left after a good year, and nothing in a bad one.
💰

Financial Lead — Key Takeaways

  • One-for-One was structural — a cost of goods sold. Grants are discretionary — a budget line. That is the whole change, and it is invisible in the impact report.
  • Twenty years and $200 million given, but the fixed cost per unit that guaranteed it is gone. Giving is now decided annually, by people who also have a P&L to hit.
  • (D) is the trap. This is not a policy, it is the absence of one. It sounds generous and guarantees that the first bad year ends the programme.
📣

Marketing Lead

“Buy a pair, give a pair” was the clearest promise in retail. What replaced it is more useful and far harder to put on a shoebox. How do you describe the company now?

  • A Report what actually changed for people, verified externally — and accept a harder story.
  • B One simple headline number, with the outcome evidence published underneath it.
  • C Go back to One-for-One — a promise people understand is worth more than one they don't.
  • D Lead with 106 million lives and $200 million given; don't draw attention to the change.
📣

Marketing Lead — Key Takeaways

  • Admit the effectiveness-versus-legibility trade-off out loud. The new model is better and less repeatable, and pretending otherwise is the failure mode.
  • “106 million lives impacted” uses a word the report never defines, and nobody outside the company audits either number.
  • (D) is the trap. Marketing a promise the company no longer keeps — the most comfortable failure in social enterprise, because nobody ever has to admit it happened.
⚙️

Operation Lead

Giving now spreads across mental health, gun violence, education and disaster relief, through a large partner roster, on roughly $10 million a year. How do you structure the portfolio?

  • A Fewer partners, bigger multi-year money, held to measured outcomes.
  • B Keep the breadth, but require the same outcome measures from every partner.
  • C Let local partners set priorities and fund them flexibly.
  • D Spread as widely as possible — more causes and countries make a bigger story.
⚙️

Operation Lead — Key Takeaways

  • Ask what a grant does if it is a rounding error to the recipient. Roughly $10 million a year across four unrelated causes buys very little in any one of them.
  • Concentration is the uncomfortable answer: fewer partners and multi-year money is how a grant becomes a change rather than a line item.
  • (D) is the trap. It optimises the report, not the outcome. More logos, thinner grants, nothing decisive anywhere.
💻

Tech Lead

The report counts dollars given and lives “impacted” — a word it never defines — and nobody outside the company audits either. What do you build?

  • A Outcome measurement with partners, checked by someone who doesn't work for us.
  • B Fund partners' own measurement capacity rather than imposing a system.
  • C Buy an off-the-shelf impact platform for numbers this year rather than in three.
  • D Keep counting what we give — cheap, honest about the input, and what customers respond to.
💻

Tech Lead — Key Takeaways

  • Separate inputs from outcomes explicitly. Dollars given is an input the company controls; lives changed is an outcome it does not.
  • Measurement is not free: every dollar spent measuring is a dollar not granted. A strong answer states that defence before rejecting it.
  • (D) is the trap. Honest about the input and silent about the result — and cheap, which is why it persists.
🔒

Security Lead

Proving outcomes means collecting data about beneficiaries — some in mental-health crisis, some in violence-prevention programmes. A leak would not embarrass the company; it could endanger them. How much do you collect?

  • A The least possible, de-identified — a leak here endangers people, not the brand.
  • B A full protection and audit programme across partners before any data moves.
  • C Leave the data with partners; write the obligations into grant agreements.
  • D Collect as much detail as possible — richer data proves the impact is real.
🔒

Security Lead — Key Takeaways

  • Reach for data minimisation before data protection. The safest record is the one you never collected.
  • The people in this dataset are in mental-health crisis and violence-prevention programmes. The severity here is measured in harm to them, not cost to the brand.
  • (D) is the trap. It sounds rigorous. It builds a list of people in crisis, spread across dozens of under-resourced nonprofits.
🧭

Account Manager

You do not answer a functional question. After all five leads present their selection and a one-sentence defence:

  • 1 Rank the five problems 1–5, each used once, with one sentence justifying #1 that contains a figure from the packet.
  • 2 Weight them — 100 points across the five, in blocks of 10, totalling exactly 100.

Part 3 — Place the company on the axis. Nonprofit ← social enterprise → corporation. Say where TOMS sits and in which direction it has been moving.

🧭

Account Manager — Key Takeaways

  • Ranking is order; weighting is magnitude. A flat split scores 2 of 6 — the designed penalty for refusing to choose.
  • The strong pattern ranks funding and measurement above story and partners, weighting those two at about 70 of 100 between them.
  • Placing TOMS on the axis is not enough — the movement is the finding. A company can keep its certification and still drift.

Key takeaway

Nobody audits an impact report. A mission that was once a cost of goods sold is now a budget line, and the difference only becomes visible in a bad year.

Dollars given is an input you control. Lives changed is an outcome you do not.
