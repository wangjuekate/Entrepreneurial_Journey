Social Entrepreneurs

MENT 2234 · Module 4 · Concept Lecture

Case: TOMS Shoes · Mental model: the Mission–Margin Axis

Warn them early and once: this is not a referendum on whether TOMS is a good company. The interesting question is structural, not moral.

The coffee shop with a mission

  • A local coffee shop hires and trains people experiencing homelessness or barriers to employment.
  • It sells coffee, sandwiches and snacks like anyone else. Customers pay. The shop earns revenue.
  • It also provides job training, mentoring and support to move people into stable employment.
  • Training new workers costs time and money. Managers must choose: efficiency, or more support for the people who need it most.

Four questions

What makes this different from a typical business?

Is its primary goal making money or helping people?

Can it successfully do both?

What challenges will show up first?

Push on the third question. The room will say "yes, obviously" — the rest of the hour is about what "successfully" has to mean for that to be true.

Between two familiar shapes

Traditional for-profit

Primary goal: profit.
Shareholder focus.
Revenue from customers.

Social enterprise

Dual goal: profit and social impact.
Multiple stakeholders.
Revenue plus mission.

Nonprofit

Primary goal: social mission.
Community beneficiaries.
Donations, grants, fundraising.

Goodwill · Greyston Bakery · TOMS · Patagonia · fair-trade cooperatives · community-owned groceries · microfinance · renewable energy co-ops. Social enterprises use business activity to address social or environmental problems while staying financially sustainable.

Brainstorm the examples on the board before revealing the strip. Whatever they name, ask which column it belongs in — the arguments are the useful part.

Sometimes the two reinforce each other

Patagonia

Environmental commitment attracts customers who pay a premium; the premium funds more of the commitment.

Greyston Bakery

Open hiring fills roles others cannot fill, which produces a workforce and a product at the same time.

Microfinance

Lending to people banks will not serve creates borrowers, repayments and a market at once.

In each case the loop closes: impact strengthens business performance, and business success buys more impact.

Do not let this land as inspiration. The next slide is the one that matters — these are the easy cases, chosen because the loop closes.

And sometimes they point in opposite directions

Price

Charging what the customer can pay serves the mission. Charging what the market will bear serves the margin.

Customer selection

The people who most need the product are usually the least able to pay for it.

Growth

Expanding reach dilutes depth. Going deeper limits how many people you touch.

Measurement

Money spent proving impact is money not spent creating it.

Honesty

The most accurate story is rarely the most persuasive one.

None of these has a clean answer

That is the point — and it is why you need a model rather than a slogan.

Ask for a real example of each from the coffee-shop story. It is a five-minute exercise and it makes the figure land immediately.

The mental model — the Mission–Margin Axis

Most textbooks stop at the axis. True, and useless — it tells you where a company sits, not whether it will still be there in ten years. The arrow underneath is the model.

Beats 1-3 build the textbook picture; go fast. Beat 4 is the turn — slow down. Beat 5 is the test, and it is the only one that discriminates between two companies at the same point on the axis.

Two companies at the same point, opposite futures

Reinforcing

Every unit sold funds the mission automatically, and doing more mission makes the product more attractive, which sells more units.

The loop closes. Nobody has to win an argument for it each year.

Working against

The mission is funded from whatever is left over. Every dollar of mission is visibly a dollar off the bottom line.

Somebody has to defend it annually, against people carrying revenue targets.

The useful question is not where a company sits. It is which of these two structures it has — and marketing cannot tell you.

Make them classify the coffee shop, Patagonia and Greyston out loud before moving on. Greyston is the cleanest reinforcing case in the set.

Why TOMS is the case that teaches this

Inside cost of goods

Giving moved with sales automatically. It was not a decision.

Nobody decided it annually

So it could not lose a budget argument.

It sold the product

Mission drove margin, not only the reverse.

Degraded gracefully

A bad year meant less giving, not no giving.

The original promise — "for every pair sold we give a new pair to a child in need" — was a reinforcing structure, and every property above is why.

Be precise here. Students remember TOMS as a marketing gimmick; the structural properties are what make it a teaching case rather than a nostalgia trip.

By 2025 the promise is retired

  • Giving is now cash grants, decided periodically — mental health, violence prevention, community health.
  • Almost everyone who studies it agrees the grants do more good per dollar than shoes people did not always need.
  • And giving has moved from the cost-of-goods line to the budget line. It is now an agenda item, argued each year — the only item in the room with no contract behind it.

Both of these are true at once

The company got better at doing good and structurally weaker at continuing to.

Sit with that. It is the whole lesson, and it is not a criticism.

Expect pushback in both directions. Hold the line that both statements are true — the discomfort is the learning, and resolving it too fast destroys the case.

The measurement problem that comes with the territory

Audited vs not

A corporation's financial numbers are audited. Somebody outside signs their name and can be sued if they are wrong.

Impact numbers are not.

From the 2025 report

"106,220,000 lives impacted" — and "impacted" is never defined anywhere in the document.

$200,000,000 given — with no revenue figure to compare it against.

Neither is dishonest. Both are unverifiable.

Input

Dollars given, pairs donated. Measures what the company did. Easy, cheap, and what customers respond to.

Outcome

What actually changed for a person. Measures what happened. Expensive, slow, and the only real evidence.

This slide decides half the simulation. The Tech Lead will want outcome data; the Security Lead is protecting people in crisis. Both are right — flag it now.

The one question the model makes you ask

Whenever someone shows you a company doing well by doing good: what happens to the mission in the first year the company loses money?

If the answer is "it shrinks proportionally" — the two reinforce.

If the answer is "someone will have to make the case for it" — they do not, whatever the marketing says.

Have them run the question on their own team venture if it has any social claim in it. Most discover they are in the right-hand box.

Two warnings

This is not a referendum on whether TOMS is a good company. It gave away $200 million and it retired its signature promise. Both are facts.

Two of your roles will disagree and both be right. Do not let them split the difference without arguing it properly.

Hand out role packets. Say the second warning to the whole room, not just to the two roles — the Account Manager has to weigh that disagreement.
