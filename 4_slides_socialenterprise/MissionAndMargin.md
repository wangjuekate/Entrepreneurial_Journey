# Mission and Margin: Can a Social Enterprise Widen Its Purpose Without Losing Its Ability to Survive?

Social entrepreneurship is usually introduced as a solution to a false choice. You do not have to pick between doing good and making money, the argument goes; design the business model so revenue and impact rise together. The materials from *The New York Times*, Anthropic, and Open to Debate support the ambition but complicate the mechanics. Mission and margin are genuinely linkable — and the link is fragile in a specific, recurring way. **The failure mode is not greed overtaking purpose. It is purpose expanding faster than the model that funds it.**

The problems these readings emphasize are structural. Anthropic's adoption research documents one clearly: the benefits of a general-purpose technology are not distributed by need. Across countries, **a 1% increase in GDP per working-age capita corresponds to roughly a 0.7% rise in Claude usage per capita**, and the gaps are severe — Indonesia at 0.36 times expected usage, India at 0.27, Nigeria at 0.2 (Appel et al., 2025, [Anthropic Research](https://www.anthropic.com/research/anthropic-economic-index-september-2025-report)). Where adoption does occur in lower-income countries its character differs, with **"coursework use cases…more common in lower-income countries"** while work and personal uses dominate wealthier ones (Appel et al., 2026, [Anthropic Research](https://www.anthropic.com/research/anthropic-economic-index-january-2026-report)).

That is a textbook social-enterprise opening. A gap exists, it is measurable, and no ordinary market actor is closing it — poorer users are less profitable to serve, so commercial incentives point away from exactly the places where marginal benefit is highest. Anthropic's institutional response is to fund research rather than to price differently: its Economic Futures program aims to **"study what programs could make the economy more flexible and resilient, ensure the benefits of AI are shared, and minimize the harm that AI-driven disruption could cause"** (Anthropic, [Economic Futures](https://www.anthropic.com/economic-futures)). Notice what that concedes. A profitable company addressing distributional harm did not find a business model for it; it created a philanthropic one. **A real social problem does not guarantee a viable venture to solve it.**

Open to Debate has hosted the underlying dispute about whether markets can do this work at all. In November 2019, Whole Foods co-founder John Mackey — a leading advocate of conscious capitalism — and Katherine Mangu-Ward of *Reason* argued that **"no other system has been as effective in creating value, increasing prosperity, and producing the wealth that has lifted billions of people out of poverty,"** against Bhaskar Sunkara of *Jacobin* and economist Richard Wolff, who countered that capitalism is **"inherently exploitative"** and **"inherently rigged to benefit the wealthy and powerful"** (Mackey, Mangu-Ward, Sunkara & Wolff, 2019, [Open to Debate](https://opentodebate.substack.com/p/capitalism-blessing)). Social enterprise is a wager that Mackey's side is close enough to right — that a firm can be redesigned to serve people it would otherwise extract from, without needing a different economic system.

The strongest cautionary evidence here concerns what happens when a mission-driven organization broadens its purpose. *The New York Times* documented the Sierra Club's expansion from environmental protection into a wider social-justice agenda. Former executive director Michael Brune framed the logic in terms most social entrepreneurs would recognize: **"We can't defend the environment by shutting ourselves up in a big, green box labeled 'environmental issues.'"** The reasoning is sound — environmental harm is not separable from who bears it.

The results, as reported, were severe. The organization lost roughly 60% of its four million members after 2019, went through three rounds of layoffs, and faced a projected $40 million deficit; labor expenses **"would double between 2016 and 2024."** A survey found members **"ranked climate change first, and racism tied for last."** In June 2025 a group of managers wrote to leadership that **"Sierra Club is in a downward spiral."** Former board president Aaron Mair named the risk precisely: **"It's almost like uprooting a sequoia and converting it into an ax handle"** (Fahrenthold & Brown, 2025, The New York Times, via [GV Wire](https://gvwire.com/2025/11/11/sierra-club-embraced-social-justice-and-then-it-tore-itself-apart/)).

Read carefully, that is not a story about a mission being wrong. It is a story about **a mission and a funding base coming apart**. Revenue came from members who had joined for a specific purpose; broadening the mission was defensible on the merits and fatal to the model, because the model depended on those particular people continuing to give. A social enterprise's business model is not just how it makes money — it describes *who is willing to pay, and for what*. Change the second without changing the first and the organization runs out of room.

This is the honest limit of social enterprise, and it cuts both ways. Tie revenue tightly to mission and you gain durability but lose the freedom to follow the problem where it leads. Keep them loosely coupled and you gain flexibility while depending on funders whose priorities may diverge from the people you serve. No configuration escapes the trade; they only fail differently.

The most important unanswered question, therefore, is:

**When measured impact and financial sustainability point in different directions, which one should a social enterprise treat as the constraint?**

Every organization in these readings faced a version of that choice. Serving the users with the greatest need is often the least commercially viable thing an enterprise can do. A venture that serves them well may not survive to serve them next year; one that survives may drift toward customers who can pay, and report excellent metrics about people who were never the point.

Measurement makes this worse. Financial sustainability has an unambiguous number attached; social impact does not. When one side of a trade-off is precisely measurable and the other contested, organizations reliably optimize the measurable side while describing themselves in terms of the other. That gap is where mission drift lives, and nothing in the reporting flags it.

The useful discipline is not choosing mission over margin or margin over mission, but **stating in advance what result would mean the enterprise had stopped being worth running** — and checking against it while there is still money left to change course.
